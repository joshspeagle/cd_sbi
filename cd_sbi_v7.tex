\section*{Abstract}

Simulation-based inference (SBI) is now the standard tool for
likelihood-free inference in scientific simulators, but its commonly
used objectives --- neural posterior, likelihood, and ratio estimation
--- do not enforce frequentist coverage. \citet{HermansEtAl2022}
documented that off-the-shelf SBI methods are often substantially
overconfident at realistic simulation budgets, with no internal
diagnostic to detect this.

This document develops an alternative framework whose training target
is, by construction, a calibrated \emph{confidence distribution} (CD) in
the Schweder--Hjort / Fraser tradition. The method learns a pivotal
quantity \(r: \Theta \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d\) such that,
under the data-generating distribution,
\(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) for every true
\(\theta_0\). The induced CD then has exact frequentist coverage at
every confidence level --- \emph{pointwise in \(\theta_0\), not merely
on average over the proposal.}

The method has three ingredients:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A standard NF-MLE objective}: per-sample maximum likelihood
  for \(r\) viewed as a conditional normalizing flow,
  \[L(r) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(\theta, X) \sim \rho \otimes P_\theta}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\|r(\theta, X)\|^2 \;-\; \log\bigl|\det \partial r/\partial X\bigr|\right].\]
  This is the same objective used by Sequential Neural Likelihood (SNL;
  \citealp{PapamakariosEtAl2019}).
\item
  \textbf{Architectural monotonicity in \(\theta\)}: \(r\) is
  parameterized so that \(\theta \mapsto r_k(\theta; X)\) is strictly
  increasing in each coordinate (autoregressively, in higher
  dimensions). This is what converts a trained flow into a valid CD
  pivot.
\item
  \textbf{Architectural monotonicity in \(X\)}: \(r\) is parameterized
  so that \(\partial r/\partial X\) is (lower-)triangular with positive
  diagonal. This is what makes the change-of-variables Jacobian both
  correct and computationally cheap, and avoids the spurious sub-optima
  that arise when monotonicity-in-\(X\) is left to autograd.
\end{enumerate}

We establish that:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In the regular continuous one-parameter case under \(C^1\) regularity,
  the NF-MLE objective combined with monotonicity-in-\(\theta\) uniquely
  recovers the Schweder--Hjort UMP-unbiased CD (Theorem A). A Lipschitz
  version handles ReLU networks under one mild side condition (Theorem
  A*).
\item
  The result lifts to any one-parameter regular exponential family with
  continuous sufficient statistic (Theorem C), and to discrete families
  via Lancaster's randomized PIT (Theorem C*).
\item
  In multivariate \(\theta\), a triangular-autoregressive construction
  gives a tractable architecture whose Jacobian factors into a product
  of \(d\) positive scalars, scaling linearly in \(d\). Within that
  architectural class, the calibration manifold reduces to the unique
  Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement (Theorem A-d).
\item
  Empirically, the method recovers the true pivot to within
  \textasciitilde1--3\% relative error on 1D and 2D location-Gaussian
  problems and on the exponential-rate model, with empirical coverage
  matching nominal levels to within 0.01--0.02 across all tested
  parameter values and confidence levels.
\end{itemize}

The framework reduces to the classical optimal CD in regular cases and
admits a tractable amortized neural implementation in cases where no
closed-form pivot is available. We document one architectural failure
mode (the autograd-Jacobian shortcut without monotone-in-\(X\)
enforcement) and its fix.

\section{Motivation: SBI and confidence distributions}\label{sec:1}

\subsection{The state of SBI}\label{subsec:1.1}

Many scientific models are \emph{implicit}: defined by a stochastic
simulator \(X \sim P_\theta\) from which one can sample data, but for
which the likelihood \(p(X \mid \theta)\) is intractable or unavailable
in closed form. Modern \emph{simulation-based inference}
\citep[SBI; for an overview see][]{CranmerEtAl2020} trains a deep-network
surrogate for the quantity of interest --- the Bayesian posterior
\(p(\theta \mid X)\), the likelihood \(p(X \mid \theta)\), or the
likelihood ratio --- from simulated pairs \((\theta, X)\).

The three dominant families are:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Neural posterior estimation} (NPE; \citealp{PapamakariosMurray2016};
  \citealp{GreenbergEtAl2019}): train a density estimator directly on
  the posterior \(\theta \mid X\).
\item
  \textbf{Neural likelihood estimation / Sequential Neural Likelihood}
  (NLE/SNL; \citealp{PapamakariosEtAl2019}): train a density estimator
  on \(X \mid \theta\), then run MCMC over the learned surrogate.
\item
  \textbf{Neural ratio estimation} (NRE; \citealp{HermansEtAl2020};
  \citealp{MillerEtAl2022}): train a binary classifier to distinguish
  \((\theta, X) \sim p(\theta, X)\) from
  \((\theta, X) \sim p(\theta)p(X)\), recovering the likelihood ratio
  via Bayes's rule.
\end{itemize}

On benchmarks with adequate simulation budgets, these methods are
accurate.

The problem is \emph{miscalibration}. \citet{HermansEtAl2022} (``A
Trust Crisis in Simulation-Based Inference?'') documented that the
major neural SBI families benchmarked there (NPE, NRE, and ABC variants)
systematically produce \emph{overconfident} posteriors at realistic
budgets --- credible intervals have empirical coverage well below
nominal. Subsequent work has tried to retrofit
calibration via conservative regularization \citep{DelaunoyEtAl2022},
differentiable coverage probabilities \citep{FalkiewiczEtAl2023},
Neyman-construction wrappers (LF2I: \citealp{DalmassoEtAl2024}; WALDO:
\citealp{MasseranoEtAl2023}), depth-based hyper-rectangles
\citep{BortolatoVentura2025}, and variational coverage corrections
\citep{PatelEtAl2023}. These methods all treat calibration as a
correction to a non-calibrated primary objective.

\subsection{A different starting point: confidence distributions}\label{subsec:1.2}

We instead change the primary target of inference. Rather than
estimating a posterior and correcting it, we aim to produce --- from the
simulator alone --- a \emph{confidence distribution}.

\textbf{What a confidence distribution is.} A confidence distribution
(CD) is the frequentist analogue of the Bayesian posterior: a
data-dependent distribution function \(H(\cdot; X)\) on \(\Theta\) whose
\(\alpha\)-quantile is the upper endpoint of an exact one-sided
\(\alpha\)-confidence interval. The modern theory traces back through
Fisher's fiducial inference \citep{Fisher1930}, \citet{Cox1958}, and
\citet{Efron1993, Efron1998}, with subsequent reviews by
\citet{SinghEtAl2007} and \citet{XieSingh2013}, to the textbook
synthesis in \citet{SchwederHjort2016}. \citet[Def.~3.1]{SchwederHjort2016} require
two properties: (i) \(H(\cdot; X)\) is a CDF on \(\Theta\) for each
\(X\), and (ii) the calibration condition
\textbf{exact coverage}: \[
H(\theta_0; X) \;\sim\; U(0,1) \quad \text{when } X \sim P_{\theta_0}, \text{ for every } \theta_0 \in \Theta.
\] Property (i) is enforced architecturally in this paper by (R1)
(\S\ref{subsec:2.2}); property (ii) is what NF-MLE drives the network
toward.

\paragraph{What is the PIT?} The \emph{probability integral transform}
is one of the workhorse identities of frequentist statistics: if \(Y\)
is a continuous random variable with CDF \(G\), then \(G(Y) \sim U(0,1)\).
(Direct check: \(P(G(Y) \le u) = P(Y \le G^{-1}(u)) = G(G^{-1}(u)) = u\)
for any \(u \in [0, 1]\).) The PIT is what makes the calibration
condition (ii) above non-circular --- instead of asking ``does
\(H(\theta_0; X)\) have the right distribution?'', we can build \(H\) by
finding any function whose distribution under \(X \sim P_{\theta_0}\) is
fully known and \(\theta_0\)-free, then composing with that function's
CDF. The function we are looking for is called a \emph{pivot}, and we
make this precise in \S\ref{sec:2}. For our purposes, the reference
distribution is the standard normal, so the relevant identity is
\(\Phi^{-1}(G(Y)) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) when \(Y \sim G\) continuous;
the formal calibration target is \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) rather than
\(U(0,1)^d\), but the two are equivalent under coordinatewise \(\Phi\).

\textbf{Astronomer-friendly comparison.} A CD plays the same role as a
posterior: a distribution-valued summary of the evidence for \(\theta\).
The difference is what calibration means. (\citealp{Fraser2011}, "Is
Bayes posterior just quick and dirty confidence?", gives an extended
discussion of where the two diverge.)

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A Bayesian posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid X)\) depends on a prior. Its
  credible intervals contain the true \(\theta_0\) with probability
  \(\alpha\) \emph{averaged over the prior} --- coverage holds when one
  integrates over the same prior used to define the posterior. There is
  generally no guarantee of frequentist coverage \emph{pointwise at a
  given \(\theta_0\)}.
\item
  A CD \(H(\theta; X)\) depends only on the model \(\{P_\theta\}\) and
  the data. Its quantile-based intervals contain \(\theta_0\) with
  probability \emph{exactly} \(\alpha\) \emph{for every fixed
  \(\theta_0\)}, the data being random.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The location-normal CD: a worked example.}
For the simplest non-trivial model \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)\),
constructing an exact CD is elementary. Consider the function
\(r^*(\theta, X) = \theta - X\). Under \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\),
write \(X = \theta_0 + Z\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\); then
\(r^*(\theta_0, X) = \theta_0 - X = -Z\), which is exactly
\(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) --- a distribution that does not depend on
\(\theta_0\). So \(r^*\) is a pivot in the classical sense. Composing
with the standard normal CDF gives
\[
H^*(\theta; X) := \Phi(\theta - X),
\]
which is a CDF in \(\theta\) for each \(X\) (property (i)) and which
satisfies the calibration condition (property (ii)) by direct
verification: \(\Phi(\theta_0 - X) = \Phi(-Z) \sim U(0, 1)\) exactly.
Inverting \(H^*\) at the levels \(\alpha/2\) and \(1 - \alpha/2\)
recovers the textbook \(z\)-interval
\([X - z_{1-\alpha/2},\, X + z_{1-\alpha/2}]\). More generally, in any
regular one-parameter exponential family with monotone likelihood ratio
in a continuous sufficient statistic, an analogous construction gives a
\emph{uniformly most powerful unbiased} (UMPU) CD; Theorem~C
(\S\ref{subsec:5.2}) is the precise statement, with the exp-family
worked development in \S\ref{sec:5}.

Our aim is to compute such a CD from a simulator alone, when no closed
form is available.

\subsection{The top-line goal and what supports it}\label{subsec:1.3}

\textbf{Top-line goal.} Produce, from a simulator alone, a function
\(r(\theta; X)\) whose induced CD \[
H_r(\theta; X) \;:=\; \Phi_d(r(\theta; X))
\] (\(\Phi_d\) = product standard normal CDF) has \emph{pointwise exact
frequentist coverage} --- every confidence level, every true
\(\theta_0\).

The object \(r\) is called a \emph{pivot}: in classical statistics, a
function of data and parameter whose distribution does not depend on the
parameter (the canonical example being \(X - \theta\) in the
location-normal model). Here we require the slightly stronger property
that its distribution under the truth is exactly
\(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\).

\textbf{Two characterizing properties.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{(C1) Calibration.} Under \(X \sim P_{\theta_0}\),
  \(r(\theta_0; X) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) for every
  \(\theta_0 \in \Theta\). Equivalently,
  \(H_r(\theta_0; X) \sim U(0,1)^d\). This is a \emph{frequentist}
  property --- no prior, no expectation over \(\theta\).
\item
  \textbf{(C2) Monotonicity in \(\theta\).} In 1D,
  \(\theta \mapsto r(\theta; X)\) is strictly increasing. In \(d > 1\),
  \(\theta_k \mapsto r_k(\theta; X)\) is strictly increasing at fixed
  \(\theta_{<k}\) and \(X\) (the autoregressive form developed in
  \S\ref{sec:6}). The autoregressive form is what makes the joint
  \(\Phi_d(r(\cdot; X))\) a Knothe--Rosenblatt-type transport with
  connected level sets; coordinate-wise monotonicity alone (with all
  other \(\theta_j\) held fixed) is insufficient in \(d > 1\).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The proposal \(\rho\) is not a prior.}
This is worth dwelling on, because it is the single most consequential
conceptual difference between CD-SBI and Bayesian SBI methods. The
training distribution \(\theta \sim \rho\), \(X \mid \theta \sim P_\theta\)
generates the simulation pairs the network sees during training, but
\(\rho\) plays \emph{no role at inference time}, when only
\(X_\mathrm{obs}\) is given. Look at property (C1) above: it asserts that
for \emph{every fixed} true \(\theta_0\), the law of \(r(\theta_0; X)\)
under \(X \sim P_{\theta_0}\) is \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\). No averaging
over \(\theta\) occurs; \(\rho\) does not appear. In the population
limit --- provided the architectural class \(\mathcal{F}\) contains the
calibration manifold and \(\rho\) has full support on the region of
inferential interest --- the resulting CD pivot is the same regardless
of which (full-support) \(\rho\) generated the training data. Under
misspecification or with an under-parameterized architecture this
independence breaks down: the population minimizer becomes the
\(\rho\)-weighted forward-KL projection onto \(\mathcal{F}\), which
\emph{does} depend on \(\rho\); we revisit this in \S\ref{subsec:11.4}.

If you find yourself thinking of \(\rho\) as a prior, you are reading
the wrong column of the training matrix. The implicit prior at inference
time is uniform on \(\Theta\); we sample \(\theta\) only to give the
network simulated data across the parameter space, the same way one
might sample synthetic training images across object types without
claiming any of those images is more probable than another.

\section{The pivot and the calibration manifold}\label{sec:2}

Section~\ref{sec:1} stated the goal: produce a function
\(r(\theta; X)\) whose distribution under the truth is exactly
\(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) (property (C1)) and which is monotone in
\(\theta\) (property (C2)). Section~\ref{sec:2} makes this goal
architecturally precise. We introduce two structural conditions, (R1)
and (R2), that any candidate \(r\) must satisfy \emph{by construction}
--- not as a consequence of training, but as a property of the network's
forward map. (R1) is the engineering form of (C2). (R2) is what makes
the change-of-variables formula valid so we can write down a likelihood
for \(r\) in the first place. The calibration manifold
\(\mathcal{M}\) is then the set of \(r\) in our architectural class
that additionally satisfy (C1); Section~\ref{sec:3} is about finding it.

\subsection{Notation and standing assumptions}\label{subsec:2.1}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) is the parameter space.
\item
  \(\mathcal{X}\) is the data space;
  \(\{P_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}\) is the parametric statistical
  model.
\item
  \(\rho\) is the \textbf{proposal distribution} on \(\Theta\), with
  full support on the region of inferential interest and finite first
  moment. It is a sampling device for generating training data,
  \emph{not} a Bayesian prior --- it has no role at inference time.
\item
  The training distribution is
  \((\theta, X) \sim \rho \otimes P_\theta\): sample
  \(\theta \sim \rho\), then \(X \mid \theta \sim P_\theta\).
\item
  \(\varphi, \Phi\) denote the scalar standard normal density / CDF;
  \(\phi_d, \Phi_d\) their \(d\)-variate analogues (product of standard
  normals).
\item
  \(\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) := \int p \log(p/q)\,d\mu\) is the
  Kullback--Leibler divergence; \(\mathrm{KL} \ge 0\) with equality iff
  \(p = q\) a.e.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The pivot, the implied CD, the calibration manifold}\label{subsec:2.2}

\paragraph{Two structural conditions on a pivot.}
Two things must hold \emph{by construction} for a measurable function
\(r: \Theta \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d\) to function as the
CD pivot we want. First, the induced CD
\(H_r(\theta; X) = \Phi_d(r(\theta; X))\) must be a CDF in \(\theta\)
for each fixed \(X\) --- so that quantile-based confidence regions
\(H_r^{-1}([\alpha/2, 1-\alpha/2])\) are well-defined connected sets;
this is what (R1) below enforces. Second, the map
\(X \mapsto r(\theta; X)\) must be invertible at each fixed \(\theta\)
--- so that the change-of-variables formula gives a valid density on
\(\mathcal{X}\) and we can write down a tractable likelihood under the
model and minimize it; this is (R2). The intuition behind each
condition, and what fails when it is dropped, is unpacked in
§§\ref{subsec:2.3}--\ref{subsec:2.4}; here we state the conditions
formally.

A \textbf{pivot} in the sense used here is a measurable function
\(r: \Theta \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d\) satisfying:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{(R1) Monotonicity in \(\theta\).} In 1D,
  \(\theta \mapsto r(\theta; X)\) is strictly increasing for every
  \(X\). In \(d > 1\), the autoregressive form
  (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\), \S\ref{sec:6}) applies: \(r_k\) depends on
  \(\theta\) only through \(\theta_{\le k}\), and
  \(\theta_k \mapsto r_k(\theta; X)\) is strictly increasing at fixed
  \(\theta_{<k}, X\). The increasing-rather-than-decreasing direction is
  a sign convention (Lemma 4.1 admits both branches; cf.~\S\ref{subsec:4.3});
  the convention pins the orientation of \(H_r\) so that larger \(H_r\)
  corresponds to larger \(\theta\).
\item
  \textbf{(R2) Invertibility in \(X\).} For each fixed \(\theta\), the
  map \(X \mapsto r(\theta; X)\) is a \(C^1\) diffeomorphism onto its
  image, with full-rank Jacobian everywhere. The Lipschitz extension
  needed for ReLU-network arguments is handled separately in
  \S\ref{subsec:4.4} via the auxiliary condition (R3): full-rank a.e.\ is
  \emph{not} equivalent to injectivity for Lipschitz maps, so
  (R2)-by-itself is the \(C^1\) statement and the Lipschitz case requires
  the uniform lower bound \(|\partial_X r| \ge c > 0\).
\end{itemize}

The \textbf{induced CD} is \[
H_r(\theta; X) \;:=\; \Phi_d(r(\theta; X)) \;\in\; (0,1)^d,
\] and the \textbf{calibration manifold} (within a function class
\(\mathcal{F}\)) is \[
\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F} \;:=\; \bigl\{r \in \mathcal{F} \,:\, \mathrm{Law}(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0) = \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) \text{ for every } \theta_0 \in \mathrm{supp}(\rho)\bigr\}.
\] Any element of \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\) satisfying (R1) is a valid
CD pivot.

\textbf{Concrete example.} In the location-normal model
\(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)\), the function
\(r^*(\theta, X) = \theta - X\) is a pivot in this sense:
\(r^*(\theta_0, X) = \theta_0 - X = -Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\),
satisfying (R1) (\(\partial_\theta r^* = +1\)) and (R2)

framework we develop is: find the analogue of \(r^*\) when no closed
form is available, by training a neural surrogate (Fig.~\ref{fig:c1}).

\subsection{The role of (R1): why monotonicity in \(\theta\) is needed}\label{subsec:2.3}

Calibration alone gives an object with the right marginal frequentist
behavior under each \(\theta_0\), but doesn't guarantee that confidence
sets are connected or interpretable. Monotonicity in \(\theta\) ensures:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(H_r(\cdot; X)\) is a proper CDF on \(\Theta\), so the
  level-\(\alpha\) confidence interval
  \(H_r(\cdot; X)^{-1}([\alpha/2, 1-\alpha/2])\) is a connected interval
  (1D) or rectangle (multivariate via the autoregressive structure).
\item
  The ``direction'' of the CD is fixed: larger \(H_r\) corresponds to
  larger \(\theta\), matching the convention that the CD's quantile
  function is the upper endpoint of a one-sided confidence interval.
\end{itemize}

Without (R1), the calibration manifold is large: any composition of
\(r\) with a measure-preserving bijection of \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) to
itself --- sign flips in each coordinate, non-monotone measure-preserving
rearrangements in 1D, and the entire orthogonal group \(O(d)\) in
\(d > 1\) (since \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) is rotation-invariant) --- stays
in \(\mathcal{M}\) (Fig.~\ref{fig:c2}). The ambiguity is infinite-dimensional in general; see
\S\ref{subsec:3.3} for the explicit characterization. (R1) in 1D
eliminates the sign-flip and non-monotone-rearrangement ambiguities. In
\(d > 1\), (R1) eliminates the coordinate-wise sign-flip ambiguity but
\emph{not} the rotational ambiguity --- the autoregressive triangular
structure of \S\ref{sec:6} is what selects the unique Knothe--Rosenblatt
representative within the rotation orbit; see \S\ref{subsec:11.1} for
the related open problem outside the autoregressive class.

\subsection{The role of (R2): why invertibility in \(X\) is needed}\label{subsec:2.4}

Invertibility in \(X\) at fixed \(\theta\) is what makes the
change-of-variables formula valid: \[
\hat p_r(X \mid \theta) \;=\; \phi_d(r(\theta, X)) \cdot \bigl|\det \partial r / \partial X\bigr|.
\] Without this, the right-hand side is not a valid density (it misses
the sum over preimages when \(X \mapsto r\) is not bijective), and any
loss built on it is mis-specified.

When \(r\) is not injective in \(X\) at fixed \(\theta\), the
change-of-variables formula requires summing over the multiple preimages
of each value, but the surrogate \(\hat p_r\) above keeps only one
preimage's contribution. The result fails to integrate to one in \(X\)
at fixed \(\theta\); consequently \(-\log \hat p_r\) is not bounded
below by the conditional entropy of \(X \mid \theta\) (the Gibbs
inequality requires a normalized density), and for architectures that
admit unbounded \(|\det \partial r / \partial X|\) in folded regions ---
e.g.\ ReLU / UMNN networks with no built-in monotonicity --- the NF-MLE
loss has no lower bound at all. The formal version is in
\S\ref{subsec:3.5}.

\paragraph{The empirical smoking gun.}
\S\ref{subsec:8.4} runs a controlled ablation: the same exponential-rate
problem trained both with the architectural (R2) enforcement and with
autograd alone. The autograd-Jacobian ablation reaches an NF-MLE loss
of \(0.56\); the true (analytically known) model reaches \(0.88\). Read
directionally: smaller NF-MLE loss looks better on its face, since
NF-MLE is a negative log-likelihood --- the truth should be the model
that fits the data best. But for any \emph{valid} normalized
\(\hat p_r\), Gibbs's inequality forces the population NF-MLE loss to be
at least the conditional entropy of \(X \mid \theta\), and the truth
achieves that entropy lower bound exactly: \(0.88\) is a floor that no
valid model can undercut. The ablation undercuts it, which is
mathematically impossible unless its \(\hat p_r\) is not a normalized
density. So the loss \(0.56 < 0.88\) is the smoking-gun evidence that
the autograd Jacobian is silently letting \(\hat p_r\) accumulate mass
greater than one in the folded regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:c3}). Enforcing (R2) architecturally
is what keeps \(\hat p_r\) inside the space of valid densities.

\section*{Part II — Loss design}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Part II — Loss design}

Part I introduced two desiderata for a pivot: calibration (C1) and
monotonicity in \(\theta\) (C2). Part II turns these into a training
objective. We will see that minimizing the per-sample negative
log-likelihood of \(X \mid \theta\) under the surrogate density implied
by \(r\) --- the standard normalizing-flow MLE objective ---
\emph{exactly} recovers the set of calibrated pivots within any
architectural class satisfying the invertibility condition (R2). After
proving this strict-propriety result, we contrast NF-MLE with four
alternative classes of loss design (Class 1--4) and explain why none
deliver pointwise frequentist calibration as cleanly.

\section{The NF-MLE objective}\label{sec:3}

\subsection{What a normalizing flow is, and the statement of the loss}\label{subsec:3.1}

\paragraph{What is a normalizing flow?}
A \emph{normalizing flow} \citep[overview:][]{PapamakariosEtAl2021} is a
neural-network-parameterized invertible map between a complicated
density and a simple reference density. Pick a base distribution that is
easy to evaluate --- for us, the standard Gaussian \(\phi_d\) on
\(\mathbb{R}^d\). Then a smooth, invertible function
\(r: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d\) implicitly defines a density on
\(\mathcal{X}\) via the change-of-variables formula:
\[
\hat p_r(X) \;=\; \phi_d\bigl(r(X)\bigr) \cdot \bigl|\det \partial r / \partial X\bigr|.
\]
The \(\phi_d(r(X))\) factor says ``how dense is the base distribution
at the image of \(X\)''; the \(|\det \partial r / \partial X|\) factor
is the local volume rescaling, which corrects for the fact that
\(r\) compresses some regions of \(\mathcal{X}\) and stretches others.
Together they make \(\hat p_r\) a properly normalized density on
\(\mathcal{X}\). Training a normalizing flow on data
\(\{X_i\}\) means choosing the parameters of \(r\) to maximize
\(\sum_i \log \hat p_r(X_i)\) --- equivalently, fitting \(r\) so that
\(r(X_i)\) looks like a sample from \(\phi_d\).

\paragraph{From flow to conditional flow to NF-MLE.}
For inference we need a \emph{conditional} flow: a family of maps
\(r(\theta, \cdot)\) parameterized by \(\theta\), one map for each
parameter value, so that
\(r(\theta, X) \mid X \sim P_\theta\) is distributed like \(\phi_d\).
The implied conditional density is
\(\hat p_r(X \mid \theta) = \phi_d(r(\theta, X)) \cdot |\det \partial r / \partial X|\),
and \emph{NF-MLE} means training the conditional flow by maximum
likelihood on simulated pairs \((\theta, X)\). Concretely, for a pivot
\(r\) satisfying (R1) and (R2), define
\[
\boxed{\;L(r) \;:=\; \mathbb{E}_{(\theta, X) \sim \rho \otimes P_\theta}\!\left[ \tfrac{1}{2}\|r(\theta, X)\|^2 \;-\; \log\bigl|\det \tfrac{\partial r}{\partial X}\bigr| \right].\;}
\]
This is just the negative log-likelihood of \(X \mid \theta\) under
\(\hat p_r\) with the irrelevant additive constant
\(\tfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi)\) dropped: the \(\tfrac{1}{2}\|r\|^2\) term
penalizes \(r(\theta, X)\) far from the origin of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (large
deviations from the Gaussian base), and the \(-\log|\det \partial r/\partial X|\)
term penalizes \(r\) for over-stretching the local geometry of
\(\mathcal{X}\).

\subsection{Why it works: strict propriety via KL non-negativity}\label{subsec:3.2}

\paragraph{What we are about to prove.}
The central claim of Part II is that NF-MLE on \(\mathcal{F}\)
\emph{exactly} recovers the calibration manifold: the population-level
minimizers of \(L\) are precisely the calibrated pivots in
\(\mathcal{F}\), nothing else. This property has a name from the
scoring-rule literature, \emph{strict propriety}: a loss \(L\) is
strictly proper for a target set \(\mathcal{M}\) within a function
class \(\mathcal{F}\) if \(\arg\min_{r \in \mathcal{F}} L(r) = \mathcal{M}\)
--- minimum attained exactly on \(\mathcal{M}\), nowhere else, and no
auxiliary penalties needed. \citet{GneitingRaftery2007} is the standard
reference. The theorem below says NF-MLE is strictly proper for the
calibration manifold.

\textbf{Theorem 3.2 (Strict propriety of NF-MLE).}\label{thm:strictprop}
\emph{Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a class of measurable functions
\(r: \Theta \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d\) every element of which
makes \(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta)\) a valid normalized density on
\(\mathcal{X}\) at each fixed \(\theta\). This holds when every element
satisfies the invertibility condition (R2) of \S\ref{subsec:2.2}, the
autoregressive analogue (R2\(^{\mathrm{auto}}\)) of \S\ref{subsec:6.1},
or the Lipschitz form (R2 + R3) of \S\ref{subsec:4.4}. Then}
\[
\arg\min_{r \in \mathcal{F}} L(r) \;=\; \mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F},
\]
\emph{where \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\) is the calibration manifold
(\S\ref{subsec:2.2}). The minimum value is the
\(\rho\)-averaged conditional entropy \(\mathbb{E}_\rho[H(X \mid \theta)]\),
attained by any element of \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\) and only by such
elements.}

\emph{Proof.}
The argument is short and turns on the KL identity. Under (R2),
\(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta) = \phi_d(r(\theta, \cdot)) \cdot |\det \partial r/\partial X|\)
is a normalized probability density on \(\mathcal{X}\) for every fixed
\(\theta\): the change-of-variables formula pushes \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\)
forward via \(r^{-1}\) and preserves total mass. Once \(\hat p_r\) is a
proper density, the standard decomposition
\(\mathbb{E}_{X \mid \theta}[-\log \hat p_r] = H(X \mid \theta) + \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, \hat p_r)\)
applies. Averaging over \(\rho\) gives
\[
L(r) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_\rho\bigl[\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(\cdot \mid \theta) \,\|\, \hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta)\bigr)\bigr] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_\rho\bigl[H(X \mid \theta)\bigr],
\]
where the second term --- the average conditional entropy --- is
independent of \(r\). The KL term is nonnegative and vanishes iff
\(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta) = p(\cdot \mid \theta)\) for
\(\rho\)-a.e.\ \(\theta\). Under (R2), that equality is in turn
equivalent to \(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\)
for \(\rho\)-a.e.\ \(\theta_0\) (the calibration condition (C1)).
Putting these together: \(L(r)\) is minimized exactly on
\(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Two things are worth flagging. First, (R2) is essential: without it,
\(\hat p_r\) is not a probability density, the KL identity in the proof
fails, and the loss becomes unbounded below; the formal version is in
\S\ref{subsec:3.5} and the empirical realization in \S\ref{subsec:8.4}.
Second, this is what is meant by \textbf{strict propriety}: a loss is
\emph{strictly proper} for a target \(\mathcal{M}\) if its
population-level minimum over the class of admissible \(r\) is attained
exactly on \(\mathcal{M}\) and nowhere else (Fig.~\ref{fig:c4}). The term comes from the
scoring-rule literature \citep{GneitingRaftery2007}; strict propriety
for the calibration manifold means we do not need auxiliary
independence or sharpness penalties, because the per-sample log-density
target already encodes both calibration \emph{and} the implied sharpness
of \(\hat p_r\) at every \(\theta\).

\subsection{The role of architectural constraints in selecting from \(\mathcal{M}\)}\label{subsec:3.3}

The calibration manifold over the \emph{unconstrained} class of pivots
is enormous. In 1D, any composition of a monotone transport
\(X \mid \theta \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) with a measure-preserving
bijection of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) to itself remains in the manifold; the
manifold is infinite-dimensional. In multivariate, rotations of a
calibrated pivot stay calibrated since \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) is
rotation-invariant.

NF-MLE selects an element of \(\mathcal{M}\) but does not by itself
identify which one; the architectural class \(\mathcal{F}\) does. We
will see:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In 1D, \emph{within the class \(\mathcal{F}_{C^1}\) of \(C^1\) pivots
  satisfying (R1) and (R2)}, \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\) is a singleton
  (Theorem A, \S\ref{sec:4}).
\item
  In multivariate, \emph{within the autoregressive triangular class}
  satisfying the corresponding multivariate (R1), (R2),
  \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\) reduces to a singleton: the
  Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement (Theorem A-d, \S\ref{subsec:6.3}).
\end{itemize}

So the slogan ``architecture picks out a unique element'' is precise:
the function-class restriction makes the population optimum unique.

SNL is sequential by default: it refits the flow on simulator outputs
drawn near the current posterior estimate, so the proposal \(\rho\)
changes between rounds. CD-SBI as developed here uses a fixed \(\rho\)
(amortized inference); at the single-round / non-sequential level the
two losses coincide. The full picture is:

\subsection{Why architectural monotonicity in \(X\) is essential}\label{subsec:3.5}

The previous subsection assumed (R2). What goes wrong without it?
The failure mode has four steps; the goal of this subsection is to
walk through them and explain why the fix has to be architectural
rather than a soft penalty.

\paragraph{Step 1: the setup.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop} got its propriety guarantee from a KL
identity that required \(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta)\) to be a valid
normalized density on \(\mathcal{X}\). That normalization in turn came
from the change-of-variables formula, which requires
\(X \mapsto r(\theta; X)\) to be a bijection: each value \(y\) in the
image is reached from exactly one \(X\), so the volume rescaling
\(|\det \partial r / \partial X|\) reads off cleanly. (R2) is what
guarantees this. The whole NF-MLE machinery sits on top of this
bijectivity.

\paragraph{Step 2: what folding does.}
Now suppose \(r\) is not bijective in \(X\) at some fixed \(\theta\) ---
the simplest example is that two distinct \(X_1, X_2\) get mapped to
the same value \(r(\theta, X_1) = r(\theta, X_2)\). The
change-of-variables formula then \emph{under-counts}: the correct push-
forward at the image point would sum the contribution from each
preimage, but the surrogate \(\hat p_r\) above keeps only one. The
surrogate is not a probability density on \(\mathcal{X}\) at fixed
\(\theta\); concretely, if we define
\(Z(\theta) := \int_\mathcal{X} \hat p_r(X \mid \theta)\,dX\), folding
drives \(Z(\theta)\) above \(1\). In practice the regions of
\(X\)-space where \(r\) folds tend to be regions where
\(|\det \partial r / \partial X|\) is large (the local volume rescaling
goes up to compensate for the under-counting), which the autograd
Jacobian during training picks up as ``high local likelihood'' --- the
loss locally drops, even though the model isn't actually matching \(p\).

\paragraph{Step 3: the mechanism for loss-below-truth.}
The \(\hat p_r\) we are computing is not normalized, but the normalized
version \(\hat p_r / Z(\theta)\) \emph{is} a probability density to which
the KL identity applies. Substituting:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{X \mid \theta}\bigl[-\log \hat p_r\bigr]
\;=\;
\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(p \,\big\|\, \hat p_r / Z(\theta)\bigr)
\;+\; H(X \mid \theta)
\;-\; \log Z(\theta).
\]
The KL term is still nonnegative. But the \(-\log Z(\theta)\) term is
negative whenever \(Z(\theta) > 1\), and it acts as a free downward
shift on the loss. Concretely, the population NF-MLE objective
satisfies
\(L(r) = \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, \hat p_r/Z)] + \mathbb{E}_\rho[H(X \mid \theta)] - \mathbb{E}_\rho[\log Z(\theta)]\).
For a normalized density (\(Z \equiv 1\)) the last term is zero and we
recover Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop}; for a folded \(r\) with
\(\mathbb{E}_\rho[\log Z(\theta)] > 0\), the loss falls below the
average conditional entropy --- by exactly that gap.

\paragraph{Step 4: the consequence and the empirical realization.}
This is not a small effect. \S\ref{subsec:8.4} runs the ablation
referenced in \S\ref{subsec:2.4}: same exponential-rate model, trained
with and without architectural (R2). The truth achieves the population
entropy lower bound, \(0.88\); the ablation reaches \(0.56\). The gap
\(0.32\) is empirically attributable to the
\(-\mathbb{E}_\rho[\log Z(\theta)]\) shift, i.e.\ the trained network
has discovered an architecture configuration where \(Z(\theta) \approx e^{0.32} \approx 1.38\)
on average. There is no penalty term that would dissuade gradient descent
from doing this: from the loss's perspective, lower is better.

\paragraph{The fix has to be architectural.}
A regularizer that penalizes deviation of \(Z(\theta)\) from \(1\) would
require estimating \(Z(\theta)\) by Monte Carlo on \(X\)-space at each
gradient step, which is expensive and noisy. The clean fix is to make
folding impossible by construction: arrange the architecture so that
\(X \mapsto r(\theta; X)\) is provably injective for every \(\theta\)
the network can express. Two parameterizations achieve this:
\emph{Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Networks} (UMNNs;
\citealp{WehenkelLouppe2019}), which represent a scalar monotone
function as the integral of a strictly positive function from a free
neural net; and \emph{autoregressive triangular flows}, where each
\(r_k\) depends only on coordinates with index \(\le k\) and is monotone
in its own coordinate. In both cases the Jacobian is closed-form and
positive by construction, so \(\hat p_r\) is a normalized density and
the strict-propriety guarantee of Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop} applies.
The architectural choices are developed in detail in \S\ref{sec:6}
(triangular flows) and \S\ref{subsec:7.1} (UMNNs).

\subsection{The design space of strictly proper objectives}\label{subsec:3.7}

The structure of the problem --- find a function \(r\) whose minimizers,
over a suitable class, are exactly the calibration manifold
\(\mathcal{M}\) --- admits multiple families of candidate losses. This
subsection situates NF-MLE within that design space and contrasts with
the choices made by other SBI methods.

\paragraph{Five classes, briefly previewed.}
A natural reader might ask: why this particular loss? What about all
the other ways to make the empirical distribution of \(\Phi(r(\theta, X))\)
look like \(U(0, 1)^d\)? We consider five candidate classes of
objective and show that exactly one delivers strict propriety for the
calibration manifold without auxiliary penalties. \textbf{Class 1}
(two-sample distance on the marginal pushforward) is strictly proper
for marginal calibration only --- it cannot tell the difference between
conditional calibration and a mixture of miscalibrated conditionals
that happen to average to uniform marginally. \textbf{Class 2}
(marginal calibration + an independence penalty) fixes Class 1 in the
population limit but is finite-sample fragile, because the independence
estimator has weak gradients and an unprincipled balance with the
marginal term. \textbf{Class 3} (stratified per-\(\theta\)-bin matching)
is strictly proper but requires \(K^d\) bins, defeating the point of
amortized inference. \textbf{Class 4} (scoring rules like CRPS or
pinball applied to the residual against \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\)) gets
the propriety direction backwards and collapses \(r\) to a constant.
\textbf{Class 5} is NF-MLE (the choice of this paper), strictly proper
on the calibration manifold by Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop}. The
subsections below unpack each class.

\emph{Brief glossary for what follows.} The \textbf{Cramér--von Mises
(CvM)} distance is \(\int (F_n(z) - F_0(z))^2\, dF_0(z)\), a strictly
proper two-sample distance between an empirical CDF \(F_n\) and a
reference CDF \(F_0\). The \textbf{energy distance} is a related
\(L^2\)-distance between CDFs in higher dimensions. \textbf{HSIC}
(Hilbert--Schmidt Independence Criterion; \citealp{GrettonEtAl2005}) is
a kernel-based test statistic that is zero iff two random variables are
independent. The \textbf{continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)} is
\(\int(F(z) - \mathbf{1}\{y \le z\})^2\,dz\), a strictly proper scoring
rule for predictive distributions; the \textbf{pinball loss} is its
quantile analogue.

\textbf{Class 1: Two-sample distance on the marginal pushforward.} Apply
any strictly proper distance --- CvM, energy distance, KL --- between
\(\mathrm{Law}(\Phi(r(\theta, X)))\) and \(U(0,1)^d\) on the joint
training distribution. Strictly proper for \emph{marginal} calibration.
\textbf{Insufficient on its own}: marginal \(\Phi(r) \sim U(0,1)\) does
not imply conditional \(\Phi(r) \mid \theta \sim U(0,1)\). Explicit
counterexample: take \(\Theta = \{-1, +1\}\) with \(\rho\) uniform on
\(\{\pm 1\}\) and \(X \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)\); the
pivot \(r(\theta, X) = \mathrm{sign}(\theta)\cdot |X - \theta|\) is a
half-normal supported on \([0, \infty)\) conditional on \(\theta = +1\)
and a negative half-normal on \((-\infty, 0]\) conditional on
\(\theta = -1\), so neither conditional law is \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\); yet
the \(1/2\)--\(1/2\) mixture is exactly \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) by
symmetry, so the marginal PIT passes. The Hermans trust-crisis pattern
lives precisely here.

\textbf{Class 2: Marginal + independence.} Augment Class 1 with an
independence term \(\mathrm{HSIC}(\theta, \Phi(r))\) or similar.
Marginal calibration plus \(\theta \perp \Phi(r)\) implies conditional
calibration, so this is \emph{population-level} strictly proper for
\(\mathcal{M}\). Empirically fragile in two ways: (i) the HSIC term has
a weak gradient signal far from the optimum compared to a per-sample
density target (a standard observation in the MMD-versus-MLE training
literature); (ii) the marginal-calibration term and the independence
term must be balanced via hyperparameters, with no principled choice.
Finite-sample HSIC estimators also have variance that scales unfavorably
with the dimension of \(\theta\), so achieving independence in the
empirical loss does not ensure independence in the underlying
distribution --- the Class-1 pathology can re-emerge at small sample
sizes.

\textbf{Class 3: Stratified / conditional matching.} Discretize
\(\Theta\) into bins; within each bin, apply a two-sample distance
between \(\Phi(r(\theta, X)) \mid \theta \in \mathrm{bin}\) and
\(U(0,1)^d\). Strictly proper for each conditional. \textbf{Doesn't
scale}: requires \(K^d\) bins in \(d\)-dimensional \(\Theta\), defeating
the entire motivation for amortized inference.

\textbf{Class 4: Scoring rules on standardized residuals.} Apply a
strictly proper scoring rule --- CRPS, pinball --- to \(r(\theta, X)\)
against the reference \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\). \textbf{Directional
propriety error.} The intuition: a proper scoring rule \(S(F, y)\)
rewards a \emph{forecast} \(F\) for being close to the distribution
that produced the observed sample \(y\). It is proper in the
forecast direction: fix where the data are coming from, vary the
forecast. Here we are doing the opposite --- we have fixed the
forecast (the standard Gaussian we want \(r\) to look like) and are
varying what the data look like (the law of \(r\) under the network's
parameters). That sign flip turns ``strictly proper'' into ``actively
incentivized to collapse'': the scoring rule, applied this way,
rewards the network for producing a sample distribution close to the
\emph{median} of the reference --- which for a symmetric distribution
like \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) is a single point. We sketch the
calculation.

Recall the standard form: a scoring rule \(S(F, y)\) is strictly proper
if for any \(G^*\), the expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim G^*}[S(F, Y)]\)
over the \emph{sample} is minimized over the \emph{forecast} \(F\) at
\(F = G^*\). Here the situation is reversed: the reference
\(F = \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) is fixed and \(Y = r(\theta, X)\) is the
learned quantity, so we are minimizing over the \emph{sample
distribution} \(G\) with \(F\) fixed, which is not the proper
direction. Concretely, using the dual ("kernel-score") form of
CRPS \citep{GneitingRaftery2007},
\(\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \mathbb{E}_{Z \sim F}|Z - y| - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{Z, Z' \sim F}|Z - Z'|\),
and taking \(Y \sim G\),
\[
\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim G}\bigl[\mathrm{CRPS}(F, Y)\bigr] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{Z \sim F,\, Y \sim G}\,|Z - Y| \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{Z, Z' \sim F}|Z - Z'|.
\]
The second term does not depend on \(G\); the first is the
\(L^1\)-energy distance contribution between \(G\) and \(F\). For
\(F = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), this is minimized over distributions \(G\) at
\(G = \delta_{m(F)}\) where \(m(F) = 0\) is the median of \(F\) (the
\(L^1\) projection of any distribution onto a point mass collapses to
the median of the reference). The CRPS-against-\(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\)
loss is therefore \emph{minimized by collapsing \(r(\theta, X)\) to a
constant at the median}, the opposite of calibration.

The same conclusion follows from the more elementary expansion
\(\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim G}[\mathrm{CRPS}(F, Y)] = \int F^2\,dz + \int G(z)(1-2F(z))\,dz\),
where both integrals are individually divergent on \(\mathbb{R}\) but
their \emph{difference} is finite and equal to the (well-defined) dual
form above; the calculation should be read modulo this regularization
(equivalently, recentering at the reference median \(0\) so both
integrals are absolutely convergent). With that proviso: the
coefficient \(1 - 2\Phi(z)\) is positive on \(z < 0\) and negative on
\(z > 0\), so the pointwise-optimal valid CDF \(G\) takes \(G(z) = 0\)
for \(z < 0\) and \(G(z) = 1\) for \(z > 0\) --- the step function for
\(\delta_0\).

(LF2I \citep{DalmassoEtAl2024} avoids this collapse by applying pinball
loss in the standard direction: the test statistic is a function of the
data and parameter whose conditional distribution under \(P_\theta\) is
fixed by the simulator, and the learned quantity is the critical-value
function. Sample distribution is given, forecast is learned --- the
proper direction --- so the Class-4 obstruction does not apply.)

\textbf{Class 5: Per-sample conditional log-density (NF-MLE).} Apply KL
on the conditional density:
\(L(r) = \mathbb{E}_{(\theta, X)}\bigl[-\log \hat p_r(X \mid \theta)\bigr]\)
where \(\hat p_r\) is the change-of-variables density implied by \(r\).
Strictly proper for \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\) by \S\ref{subsec:3.2}. Scales
pointwise to any dimension. Identical to the SNL loss. \textbf{The only
subtlety is the architectural monotonicity-in-\(X\) requirement} (\S\ref{subsec:3.5})
--- without it, \(\hat p_r\) is not a valid density and the propriety
argument breaks.

\subsubsection*{Position in the SBI literature}

The five classes intersect existing SBI methods as follows. NPE/SNPE
\citep{PapamakariosMurray2016, GreenbergEtAl2019} minimize per-sample
log-density of \(\theta \mid X\) --- a Class-5 loss on the
\emph{posterior}, with no built-in coverage. NLE/SNL
\citep{PapamakariosEtAl2019} minimize per-sample log-density of
\(X \mid \theta\) --- Class-5 on the \emph{likelihood}, which is the
same loss CD-SBI uses; the difference is the architectural prescription
(monotone in \(\theta\) + monotone in \(X\)). NRE
\citep{HermansEtAl2020} targets the ratio via binary classification ---
a Class-5-flavored loss on a different object. Balanced NRE
\citep{DelaunoyEtAl2022} and Calibrated NPE \citep{FalkiewiczEtAl2023}
add regularization terms to address conservativeness post hoc; CD-SBI
achieves calibration by construction instead. LF2I
\citep{DalmassoEtAl2024}, WALDO \citep{MasseranoEtAl2023}, and Box CD
\citep{BortolatoVentura2025} operate in a structurally different mode
--- they learn
test statistics (or hyper-rectangular acceptance regions) and invert via
Neyman construction; the calibration target is the CDF of the test
statistic under each \(\theta\), not the CDF of the parameter under
data. CD-SBI's contribution within this taxonomy is the architectural
recipe that converts Class-5/SNL into a procedure whose output is a
frequentist CD by construction.

\section*{Part III — Theory in one parameter}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Part III — Theory in one parameter}

Part II showed that NF-MLE on a class \(\mathcal{F}\) recovers the
calibration manifold \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\). That is a population
statement about \emph{a set} of functions. Part III asks: how big is
that set? In 1D, within a natural \(C^1\) class, the answer is that
\(\mathcal{M}\) is a \emph{singleton} --- and that singleton is the
classical Schweder--Hjort UMP-unbiased confidence distribution.
Theorem A proves this for the location-normal model; Theorem A\(^*\)
extends to Lipschitz architectures (e.g.\ ReLU networks) under one
extra condition; Theorem C lifts both to regular one-parameter
exponential families with monotone likelihood ratio; Theorem C\(^*\)
handles discrete data via auxiliary randomization.

\section{Uniqueness in the location-normal model}\label{sec:4}

We work in 1D (\(d = 1\)) and develop the theory for
\(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)\), then lift to general regular
exponential families in \S\ref{sec:5}.

\subsection{Statement}\label{subsec:4.1}

\paragraph{What we are about to prove.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop} of Part II gave us a \emph{set} of optimal
pivots: NF-MLE on \(\mathcal{F}\) selects \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\)
(the calibrated subset of \(\mathcal{F}\)) and nothing else. The
question Part III answers is: how big is \(\mathcal{M}_\mathcal{F}\)?
For the location-normal model in 1D, restricted to a natural \(C^1\)
class, the answer is \emph{singleton}. The unique element is the same
\(r^*(\theta, X) = \theta - X\) that we wrote down by hand at the end
of \S\ref{subsec:1.2} --- so the framework, properly architected, picks
out the classical answer rather than something exotic. The strategy is
simple: show that any candidate \(r \in \mathcal{M} \cap \mathcal{F}_{C^1}\)
is, at each fixed \(\theta_0\), a strictly monotone function pushing
\(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) forward to \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) (the
calibration condition), then use the rigidity of 1D monotone
rearrangements to pin it down.

\textbf{Theorem A (Uniqueness, \(C^1\)).} \emph{Let
\(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)\) on \(\Theta = \mathbb{R}\). Within the
class} \[
\mathcal{F}_{C^1} \;:=\; \bigl\{ r : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R} \text{ satisfying (R1), (R2), with } X \mapsto r(\theta_0; X) \in C^1(\mathbb{R}) \text{ for each } \theta_0 \bigr\},
\] \emph{the calibration manifold is a singleton:} \[
\mathcal{M} \cap \mathcal{F}_{C^1} \;=\; \{r^*\}, \qquad r^*(\theta; X) = \theta - X.
\] \emph{Equivalently, \(L(r)\) over \(\mathcal{F}_{C^1}\) has a unique
minimizer, \(r = r^*\), with \(H_{r^*}(\theta; X) = \Phi(\theta - X)\)
--- the Schweder--Hjort UMP-unbiased CD.}

The proof rests on three lemmas, each carrying one piece of the
argument.

\subsection{The three lemmas}\label{subsec:4.2}

\paragraph{Three ingredients.} The proof of Theorem A needs three
separate facts. Lemma~4.1 is a measure-theoretic uniqueness result:
between two atomless 1D distributions, there is exactly one strictly
\emph{monotone-increasing} map that pushes one onto the other (and one
strictly \emph{monotone-decreasing} map). Lemma~4.2 is a rigidity
result: among \(C^1\) maps that push a Gaussian onto another Gaussian,
none are non-monotone --- non-monotone candidates produce a divergence
in the pushforward density at a critical point, which contradicts the
finiteness of the target Gaussian density. Lemma~4.3 is the propriety
half of Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop} of Part II, specialized to the
\(C^1\) class: any NF-MLE minimizer is calibrated. The Theorem A proof
combines them in the order 4.3 (calibration), 4.2 (monotonicity), 4.1
(closed form).

\textbf{Lemma 4.1 (Monotone transport).} \emph{Let
\(\theta_0 \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\psi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\)
strictly monotone such that \(\psi\) pushes \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\)
forward to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Then \(\psi(X) = X - \theta_0\) (if
strictly increasing) or \(\psi(X) = \theta_0 - X\) (if strictly
decreasing).}

\emph{Proof.} Standard 1D monotone rearrangement \citep[ch.~2]{Villani2003}:
between two atomless probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\), the unique
monotone-increasing measure-preserving map is the composition of CDF
and inverse CDF. For
strictly increasing \(\psi\): \(\Phi(\psi(X)) = \Phi(X - \theta_0)\)
a.s. by uniqueness, so \(\psi(X) = X - \theta_0\). For strictly
decreasing \(\psi\),
\(\Phi(\psi(X)) = 1 - \Phi(X - \theta_0) = \Phi(\theta_0 - X)\), so
\(\psi(X) = \theta_0 - X\). \(\blacksquare\)

\textbf{Lemma 4.2 (Level-set rigidity, \(C^1\)).} \emph{Let
\(\theta_0 \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\psi \in C^1(\mathbb{R})\) pushing
\(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Then \(\psi'\) has
constant sign on \(\mathbb{R}\); i.e., \(\psi\) is strictly monotone.}

\emph{Proof.} Suppose for contradiction \(\psi'\) changes sign on
\(\mathbb{R}\). By continuity, there is some \(X_*\) at which \(\psi'\)
crosses zero, and \(\psi\) attains a strict local extremum at \(X_*\)
(WLOG a local maximum with \(v_* := \psi(X_*)\); the local-minimum case
is symmetric). For \(v\) just below \(v_*\), the level set
\(\{X : \psi(X) = v\}\) contains at least two points \(X_1(v) \to X_*^-\)
and \(X_2(v) \to X_*^+\), with \(\psi\) strictly monotone on each of
\((X_1, X_*)\) and \((X_*, X_2)\).

The pushforward density of \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) under \(\psi\)
at any value \(v\) with finite preimage is
\[
g_\psi(v) \;=\; \sum_{i\,:\,\psi(X_i)=v} \frac{\varphi(X_i - \theta_0)}{|\psi'(X_i)|},
\]
so summing over the two preimages above,
\[
g_\psi(v) \;\geq\; \frac{\varphi(X_1(v) - \theta_0)}{|\psi'(X_1(v))|} + \frac{\varphi(X_2(v) - \theta_0)}{|\psi'(X_2(v))|}.
\]
Let \(2m \ge 2\) be the order of the leading nonzero derivative of
\(\psi\) at \(X_*\), so \(\psi(X) - v_* \sim -c\,(X - X_*)^{2m}\) near
\(X_*\) with \(c > 0\). Then
\(|\psi'(X_i(v))| \sim |v - v_*|^{(2m-1)/(2m)}\), while
\(\varphi(X_i(v) - \theta_0) \to \varphi(X_* - \theta_0) > 0\). Hence
\(g_\psi(v)\) diverges at least as fast as \(|v - v_*|^{-1/2}\) (and
faster for higher-order critical points). But the target density
\(\varphi(v_*)\) is finite, a contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

\emph{Remark (general source).} The proof uses only two facts about
\(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\): the source has
positive density at any critical point of \(\psi\), and the target has
finite density at all attained values. The lemma therefore applies
verbatim when \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) is replaced by any continuous
distribution on \(\mathbb{R}\) with positive density on its support and
\(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) by any continuous distribution with finite density.
This generalization is invoked silently in Theorem~C (\S\ref{subsec:5.2}, with the
pushforward of \(T(X) \mid \theta_0\) as source) and in the inductive
step of Theorem~A-d (\S\ref{subsec:6.3}, with the conditional law
\(F_k^{(\theta)}(\cdot \mid X_{<k})\) as source).

\textbf{Lemma 4.3 (NF-MLE is strictly proper, conditional).} \emph{Under
the NF-MLE objective \(L(r)\), any minimizer \(r \in \mathcal{F}_{C^1}\)
satisfies \(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) for
\(\rho\)-a.e. \(\theta_0\).}

\emph{Proof.} From \S\ref{subsec:3.2}, \(L(r)\) is the expected KL between
\(p(\cdot \mid \theta)\) and \(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta)\), up to a
constant. KL is zero iff
\(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta) = p(\cdot \mid \theta)\) a.e. on
\(\mathrm{supp}\,\rho\). Under (R2), \(\hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta)\)
pushes \(X \mid \theta\) to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) iff
\(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). \(\blacksquare\)

\emph{Remark (Lipschitz / autoregressive variants).} The proof depends
only on (R2) and the KL identity of \S\ref{subsec:3.2}; it therefore
extends without change to any pivot class for which the change-of-variables
formula yields a valid normalized density --- in particular to the
Lipschitz form of (R2) used in Theorem A* (\S\ref{subsec:4.4}) and to the
autoregressive variant (R2\(^{\mathrm{auto}}\)) used in \S\ref{sec:6}.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem A}\label{subsec:4.3}

\emph{Existence.} \(r^*(\theta; X) = \theta - X\) is \(C^\infty\). Under
\(X = \theta_0 + Z\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\),
\(r^*(\theta_0; X) = -Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), so
\(r^* \in \mathcal{M}\). And \(\partial_\theta r^* = 1 > 0\) (R1),
\(\partial_X r^* = -1 \neq 0\) (R2).

\emph{Uniqueness.} Let \(r \in \mathcal{M} \cap \mathcal{F}_{C^1}\). For
each \(\theta_0 \in \mathbb{R}\), define
\(\psi_{\theta_0}: X \mapsto r(\theta_0; X)\). We will pin down
\(\psi_{\theta_0}\) in three steps.

\emph{Step 1: calibration.} Since \(r\) is in the calibration manifold,
Lemma~4.3 tells us \(\psi_{\theta_0}\) pushes \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\)
forward to \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). At this point we know
\(\psi_{\theta_0}\) is a measure-preserving map between two specified
Gaussians, but nothing else about its shape.

\emph{Step 2: monotonicity.} The \(C^1\) hypothesis on
\(\psi_{\theta_0}\) plus the calibration property of Step 1 puts us in
the hypotheses of Lemma~4.2: \(\psi_{\theta_0}\) is strictly monotone.
This is the substantive step --- it rules out, e.g., a
\(\psi_{\theta_0}\) that folds the real line.

\emph{Step 3: pinning down the form.} Lemma~4.1 then applies: the only
strictly monotone maps pushing \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) to
\(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) are \(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) = X - \theta_0\) (the
increasing branch) and \(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) = \theta_0 - X\) (the
decreasing branch).

\emph{Selecting the direction.} A priori, (R1) does not rule out a
measurable mixture in which \(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) = X - \theta_0\) on
some set \(A \subseteq \Theta\) and \(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) = \theta_0 - X\)
on \(A^c\). But for any fixed \(X\), such a mixture would make
\(\theta \mapsto r(\theta, X) = \pm(\theta - X) + (\mathrm{const})\)
decreasing on \(A\) and increasing on \(A^c\), violating the global
strict monotonicity required by (R1). Hence one branch holds
\(\rho\)-a.e. The increasing-branch case \(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) = X - \theta_0\)
gives \(r(\theta, X) = X - \theta\) with \(\partial_\theta r = -1\),
contradicting (R1). The other branch
\(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) = \theta_0 - X\) gives \(r(\theta, X) = \theta - X\)
with \(\partial_\theta r = +1\), satisfying (R1). The full-support
assumption on \(\rho\) (\S\ref{subsec:2.1}) lifts this from \(\rho\)-a.e.
\(\theta_0\) to all of \(\mathbb{R}\). \(\blacksquare\)

\subsection{Strengthening to Lipschitz: Theorem A*}\label{subsec:4.4}

\paragraph{Why a separate theorem.}
Theorem A's hypothesis class is \(\mathcal{F}_{C^1}\): \(r\) is \(C^1\)
in \(X\) at each fixed \(\theta\). Many architectures used in practice
--- in particular, vanilla ReLU multilayer perceptrons --- are
Lipschitz but only \emph{piecewise} \(C^1\): they have non-differentiable
kinks at the boundaries of the ReLU regions. Theorem A's proof breaks
in this case, specifically at Lemma~4.2: the pushforward-density
divergence that produced the contradiction relies on the
quadratic-or-higher-order vanishing of \(\psi'\) at a critical point,
which is a \(C^1\) phenomenon. A Lipschitz V-shape gives only a
\emph{bounded} jump in the pushforward density, not a divergence, so
\(\psi\) can fold without contradiction. Theorem A* recovers the same
conclusion in the Lipschitz class but needs to assume the monotonicity
condition that Lemma~4.2 had been proving for free.

Lemma 4.2 uses \(C^1\) regularity essentially: the contradiction comes
from a \(|v - v_*|^{-1/2}\) (or faster) divergence of the pushforward
density near a critical value, which fails for Lipschitz V-shapes. For
ReLU networks (Lipschitz, piecewise affine), we need an alternative.

The clean approach is to require strict \(X\)-monotonicity as an extra
condition:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{(R3) {[}strict \(X\)-monotonicity, a.e.{]}:} \(\partial_X r\)
  has constant sign and is bounded away from zero on the
  differentiability set: \(|\partial_X r(\theta; X)| \geq c > 0\) a.e.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Theorem A* (Uniqueness, Lipschitz).} \emph{Suppose
\(r: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) is Lipschitz and satisfies (R1),
(R2), (R3). If \(r \in \mathcal{M}\) then \(r(\theta; X) = \theta - X\)
a.e.}

\emph{Proof.} By Rademacher's theorem (a Lipschitz function on
\(\mathbb{R}^n\) is differentiable Lebesgue-a.e.), \(r\) is
differentiable a.e. The Lipschitz form of (R2) plus (R3) makes
\(X \mapsto r(\theta_0; X)\) a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism onto its
image, so the change-of-variables formula applies a.e.\ and Lemma~4.3
extends to this class (see the Lipschitz / autoregressive remark
following Lemma~4.3 in \S\ref{subsec:4.2}). Thus
\(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) := r(\theta_0; X)\) pushes
\(\mathcal{N}(\theta_0, 1)\) to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). By (R3),
\(\psi_{\theta_0}\) is strictly monotone in \(X\). By Lemma 4.1 (1D
monotone rearrangement, valid for any pair of atomless probability
measures on \(\mathbb{R}\)),
\(\psi_{\theta_0}(X) \in \{X - \theta_0,\, \theta_0 - X\}\) a.e. Sign
determination as in Theorem A. \(\blacksquare\)

When is (R3) automatic? Whenever the architecture enforces it. For
\(C^1\) networks (tanh, GELU, softplus activations), Lemma 4.2 derives
it from \(C^1\) + calibration. For UMNN architectures with \(X\) as a
monotone input, by construction. For triangular flows of the kind
described in \S\ref{sec:6}, by construction. For vanilla ReLU networks with no
architectural enforcement, (R3) may fail, and architectural enforcement
is the recommended fix.

\subsection{The Schweder--Hjort bridge}\label{subsec:4.5}

A \textbf{UMP-unbiased CD} is, in the classical sense, the CD whose
associated one-sided test family at each level is uniformly most
powerful within the class of unbiased tests; this is the analogue of the
UMPU confidence interval in classical testing theory. For
\(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1)\), \citet[ch.~5]{SchwederHjort2016} give
the UMP-unbiased CD as
\(C^*_\mathrm{SH}(\theta; X) = \Phi(\theta - X)\). Our optimal pivot
\(r^*(\theta; X) = \theta - X\) induces
\(H_{r^*}(\theta; X) = \Phi(\theta - X) = C^*_\mathrm{SH}\). So:

\textbf{Proposition 4.5.} \emph{In the location-normal model, the unique
element of \(\mathcal{M} \cap \mathcal{F}_{C^1}\) produced by CD-SBI is
identically the Schweder--Hjort UMP-unbiased CD.}

This is what we mean by ``the framework reduces to the classical theory
in regular cases.''

\section{Lift to regular one-parameter exponential families}\label{sec:5}

\subsection{Setup and canonical pivot}\label{subsec:5.1}

\emph{Background, brief (see \citealp[\S3.4]{LehmannRomano2005} for a
textbook treatment).} A \emph{one-parameter exponential family} is a
parametric model whose density takes the canonical form
\(p(X \mid \theta) = h(X) \exp(\theta \cdot T(X) - A(\theta))\), with
\(T(X)\) a real-valued \textbf{sufficient statistic} for \(\theta\) ---
meaning \(X\) enters the likelihood only through \(T(X)\). The family
has \textbf{monotone likelihood ratio} (MLR) in \(T\) if for any
\(\theta_1 < \theta_2\), \(p(X \mid \theta_2)/p(X \mid \theta_1)\) is a
monotone function of \(T(X)\). This is automatic in the canonical form
when \(T\) is real-valued. MLR is the hypothesis that powers most
classical optimal-testing results.

Now let \(\{P_\theta : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\}\) be a regular
one-parameter exponential family with sufficient statistic
\(T : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\). Assume \(T(X)\) has a continuous
positive density under each \(P_\theta\), and assume increasing MLR.
(The decreasing-MLR case differs only in sign convention.)

The \textbf{canonical CD pivot} is \[
r^*(\theta; X) \;:=\; \Phi^{-1}\bigl(1 - F_\theta(T(X))\bigr),
\] where \(F_\theta\) is the CDF of \(T(X)\) under \(P_\theta\). (For
decreasing MLR, drop the \(1 -\).)

\emph{Verification.} Under \(X \sim P_{\theta_0}\),
\(F_{\theta_0}(T(X)) \sim U(0,1)\) by the probability integral
transform, so \(r^*(\theta_0; X) \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). (R1) holds by
MLR (the CDF \(F_\theta\) is monotone-decreasing in \(\theta\) at any
fixed \(t\) under increasing MLR). (R2) holds since \(r^*\) is strictly
monotone in \(T(X)\) and we assume the density of \(T(X)\) is positive
on its support.

\subsection{Theorem C: uniqueness}\label{subsec:5.2}

\paragraph{What we are about to prove, and why the lift is easy.}
Theorem A pinned down the calibration manifold to a singleton in the
location-normal case. Theorem C extends the same conclusion to any
regular one-parameter exponential family with MLR. The proof works
because the manuscript's three-lemma argument never used anything
specific about Gaussian shape beyond two facts: the source measure has
a continuous positive density, and the target measure has a finite
density. The exponential-family setup gives us both: by sufficiency, we
can work in \(t = T(X)\) coordinates where the source is
\(g_{T \mid \theta_0}\) (continuous positive density on its support by
the hypotheses of \S\ref{subsec:5.1}), the target is still the
standard Gaussian, and the three lemmas of \S\ref{subsec:4.2} apply
verbatim. The remarks on Lemma~4.2 (general source) and Lemma~4.3
(Lipschitz / autoregressive variants) cover exactly this transfer.

\textbf{Theorem C (Lift to exponential families).} \emph{Under the setup
of \S\ref{subsec:5.1}, within the class
\(\mathcal{F}^T_{C^1} := \{r : t \mapsto r(\theta_0; T^{-1}(t)) \in C^1 \text{ for each } \theta_0\}\),
we have \(\mathcal{M} \cap \mathcal{F}^T_{C^1} = \{r^*\}\).}

\emph{Proof.} Work in \(t = T(X)\) coordinates. The pushforward density
\(g_{T \mid \theta_0}(t)\) is continuous and positive on its support
by the hypotheses of \S\ref{subsec:5.1}. The three-lemma argument of
Theorem A then applies in this t-coordinate system:
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item Lemma 4.3 (Lipschitz / autoregressive remark, \S\ref{subsec:4.2})
  gives \(t \mapsto \tilde r(\theta_0; t)\) pushes
  \(g_{T \mid \theta_0}\) forward to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), for
  \(\rho\)-a.e.\ \(\theta_0\), under the NF-MLE / (R2) hypotheses.
\item Lemma 4.2 (general source remark, \S\ref{subsec:4.2}) applies with
  source \(g_{T \mid \theta_0}\) (continuous positive density on
  \(\mathrm{supp}(T)\)) and target \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (continuous
  finite density), yielding strict monotonicity in \(t\).
\item Lemma 4.1 then identifies \(\tilde r(\theta_0; t)\) as one of two
  monotone branches mapping \(g_{T \mid \theta_0}\) to
  \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\); the increasing branch under increasing MLR is
  \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - F_{\theta_0}(t))\), giving \(r^*\). Direction is fixed
  by MLR.
\end{itemize}
\(\blacksquare\)

\subsection{Worked examples}\label{subsec:5.3}

\emph{An \textbf{ancillary statistic} is one whose distribution does not
depend on the parameter; conditioning on an ancillary loses no
information and is the standard tool for reducing nuisance dimensions in
classical inference \citep{Cox1958}.}

\textbf{Location-normal with unknown variance.}
\(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \sigma^2)\) iid, both unknown.
Conditioning on the ancillary \(S^2 := \sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2\), the
conditional model is a one-parameter location problem with pivot \[
r^*(\theta; \bar X, S) \;=\; \Phi^{-1}\bigl( F_{t_{n-1}}\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,(\theta - \bar X)/S\bigr) \bigr).
\] This is the Student-\(t\) CD.

\textbf{Exponential rate.} \(X_i \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\theta)\) iid,
\(\theta > 0\) rate. Sufficient statistic
\(T = \sum_i X_i \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(n, \theta)\), with
\(2\theta T \sim \chi^2_{2n}\). MLR is decreasing in \(\theta\) (larger
\(\theta\) $\Rightarrow$ smaller mean of \(T\)), so \[
r^*(\theta; T) \;:=\; \Phi^{-1}\bigl( F_{\chi^2_{2n}}(2\theta T)\bigr).
\] This is the test case for whether the framework handles non-additive
pivots (it does, with the right architecture; see \S\ref{subsec:8.4}).

\setcounter{subsection}{6}
\subsection{Discrete extension via randomization}\label{subsec:5.7}

For discrete sufficient statistics (T(X) countable), no function of
\(T(X)\) alone can have a continuous distribution, so the strict
calibration target \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) is unattainable. The standard
fix --- introduced in the fiducial tradition by
\citet{Stevens1950} and used in the form below by \citet{Lancaster1961}
--- is auxiliary randomization: smear each atom across
an interval of an auxiliary uniform variable, so that the combined
object is continuous.

\subsubsection{Randomized PIT}\label{subsubsec:5.7.1}

\paragraph{The intuition.}
The probability integral transform of \S\ref{subsec:1.2} only works for
continuous random variables --- the identity \(G(Y) \sim U(0, 1)\) needs
\(G\) continuous, otherwise \(G(Y)\) lives on the discrete set of CDF
values and cannot be uniform. So if \(T(X)\) is discrete, no
deterministic function of \(T(X)\) alone can have the continuous
calibration target \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). The fix is to add randomness:
spread each atom of \(T(X)\) across an interval of length equal to that
atom's probability, using an independent uniform \(U\). Concretely, the
\(t\)-th atom of \(T(X)\) (with probability mass \(p_\theta(t)\) and
cumulative \(F_\theta(t^-)\) below it) gets stretched into the
sub-interval \([F_\theta(t^-),\, F_\theta(t^-) + p_\theta(t)]\), with
each point in the sub-interval indexed by the value of \(U\). The
resulting "filled-in" random variable is uniform on \([0, 1]\) by
construction, even though \(T(X)\) itself is discrete.

\paragraph{The formula.}
Let \(X \sim P_\theta\) discrete with sufficient statistic \(T(X)\) and
MLR in \(\theta\). Define \(F_\theta(t) = P_\theta(T \leq t)\),
\(F_\theta(t^-) = P_\theta(T < t)\), \(p_\theta(t) = P_\theta(T = t)\).
Introduce \(U \sim U(0,1)\) independent of \(X\). The \textbf{randomized
PIT} is \[
V_\theta(X, U) \;:=\; F_\theta(T(X)^-) + U \cdot p_\theta(T(X)).
\] \emph{Fact.} Under \(X \sim P_{\theta_0}\), \(U \sim U(0,1)\)
independent: \(V_{\theta_0}(X, U) \sim U(0,1)\) exactly.

\emph{Quick worked example.} Take \(X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(1, \theta)\)
with \(\theta = 0.3\), so \(p_\theta(0) = 0.7\) and \(p_\theta(1) = 0.3\).
The randomized PIT spreads the \(X = 0\) atom across \([0, 0.7]\) and
the \(X = 1\) atom across \([0.7, 1]\):
\[
V_\theta(0, U) = 0.7\,U \in [0, 0.7],
\qquad
V_\theta(1, U) = 0.7 + 0.3\,U \in [0.7, 1].
\]
Each conditional distribution \(V_\theta(t, U) \mid T = t\) is uniform
on its sub-interval. Sampling \(X\) then \(U\) gives, for any \(v \in [0, 1]\),
\(P(V_\theta(X, U) \le v) = 0.7 \cdot \min(v / 0.7, 1) + 0.3 \cdot \max(0, (v - 0.7)/0.3)\)
\(= v\) --- i.e.,
\(V_\theta(X, U) \sim U(0, 1)\), as the Fact asserts. The composition
\(r^*_\mathrm{rand}(\theta; X, U) := \Phi^{-1}(1 - V_\theta(X, U))\)
then has the desired \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) calibration.

The CD-SBI framework lifts to this setting by enlarging the input space:
the pivot becomes
\(r: \Theta \times \mathcal{X} \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}\), training
data are triples \((\theta_i, X_i, U_i)\), and the canonical pivot is \[
r^*_\mathrm{rand}(\theta; X, U) \;:=\; \Phi^{-1}(1 - V_\theta(X, U)).
\]

\subsubsection{Theorem C* (Discrete uniqueness)}\label{subsubsec:5.7.2}

\paragraph{What this theorem accomplishes.}
The continuous-case uniqueness theorems (A, A\(^*\), C) had three
regularity conditions: (R1) on \(\theta\), (R2) on \(X\), and (when
needed) (R3) on the Lipschitz Jacobian. In the discrete setting we
gain a fourth, (R4), to pin down the ordering of \(T\)-atoms relative
to the auxiliary uniform \(U\). The reason: in the continuous case, the
calibration condition plus monotonicity-in-\(X\) (from (R1) and (R2)
together with Lemma~4.2's rigidity) already forces a unique
\emph{ordering} on \(X\)-space; in the discrete setting, the atoms of
\(T\) can be \emph{permuted} without breaking calibration or
within-atom monotonicity-in-\(U\), and (R4) is the condition that rules
out such permutations.

Uniqueness in the randomized class therefore requires four regularity
conditions: (R1) monotone in \(\theta\), (R2) invertible in \((X, U)\),
(R3\(_U\)) strict monotone in \(U\) (distinct from (R3) of
\S\ref{subsec:4.4}: that condition was a uniform lower bound
\(|\partial_X r| \ge c > 0\) used in the Lipschitz case; (R3\(_U\))
here is the strict-monotone-in-\(U\) condition needed inside each
\(T\)-atom in the discrete setting), plus a new condition:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{(R4) Order-coherence in \(T(X)\):} for fixed \((\theta, U)\),
  \(V_\theta(X_1, U) \le V_\theta(X_2, U)\) whenever
  \(T(X_1) < T(X_2)\) --- equivalently, \(r(\theta; X_1, U) \ge r(\theta; X_2, U)\),
  with strict inequality somewhere. (Note: the canonical \(r^*_\mathrm{rand} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - V_\theta)\)
  is \emph{decreasing} in \(T(X)\) under increasing MLR, since \(V^*_\theta\)
  tiles \([0, 1]\) in increasing \(T\)-order; (R4) is the discrete analogue
  of this directionality.)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Theorem C* (Discrete).} \emph{Under (R1)--(R4), the unique pivot
in the randomized calibration manifold is \(r^*_\mathrm{rand}\).}

\emph{Proof.} For each fixed \((\theta, t)\) with \(t \in \mathrm{supp}(T)\),
(R3\(_U\)) makes \(U \mapsto V_\theta(X(t), U)\) strictly monotone, and
(R2) makes it a bijection from \([0,1]\) to a sub-interval of \([0,1]\)
of length \(p_\theta(t)\). (R4) positions this sub-interval immediately
above the corresponding sub-interval for any \(t' < t\) in
\(\mathrm{supp}(T)\). Stacking these sub-intervals in increasing
\(t\)-order (the lex order \((T, U)\)) is the unique tiling that
(i) preserves the marginal mass \(p_\theta(t)\) of each atom, (ii)
respects the within-atom monotonicity from (R3\(_U\)) and (R4)'s
cross-atom order, and (iii) covers \([0,1]\) without overlap. That
tiling is exactly \(V^*_\theta\). The composition
\(r = \Phi^{-1}(1 - V_\theta)\) is then uniquely \(r^*_\mathrm{rand}\),
with the sign of \(\Phi^{-1}\)'s composition fixed by (R1).

Dropping any one of (R1)--(R4) breaks uniqueness: without (R1), the
\(\theta\)-direction is ambiguous; without (R2), \((T, U) \mapsto V\) is
not a bijection onto \([0,1]\); without (R3\(_U\)), the within-atom
order is unconstrained; without (R4), a different tiling that
interleaves \(T\)-atoms across \(U\) gives an alternative calibrated
pivot. \(\blacksquare\)

\textbf{Why (R4) is genuinely needed: an explicit counterexample.}
A skeptical reader might suspect that (R4) is redundant given
(R1), (R2), (R3\(_U\)) --- isn't strict monotonicity in \(U\) enough,
once we have the rest? The answer is subtle: redundancy holds for
small atom counts but fails at \(\mathrm{Bin}(3, \theta)\) due to an
algebraic accident in the binomial coefficients. We work through the
small cases briefly to show why (R4) is not just decoration, then
exhibit the counterexample.

A \(\mathrm{Bin}(1, \theta)\) construction is too small to separate
(R4) from the other conditions: any monotone-in-\(U\) completion of an
alternative \(V'_\theta\) on a binary support preserves (R4), and any
completion that violates (R4) breaks (R3\(_U\)) via a downward jump. A
\(\mathrm{Bin}(2, \theta)\) construction also fails to separate (R4):
each of the six permutations of \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) other than the
canonical one has at least one endpoint of the form
\(\sum_{t \in S} p_\theta(t)\) for a non-prefix subset \(S\), and direct
calculation shows none of those endpoint functions is non-increasing
in \(\theta\) --- so (R1) already rules out the swaps before (R4) is
even considered.

The smallest model where (R4) is genuinely independent is
\(\mathrm{Bin}(3, \theta)\), with atoms
\(\{0, 1, 2, 3\}\) and masses
\(p_\theta(t) = \binom{3}{t}\theta^t(1-\theta)^{3-t}\). The canonical
\(V^*_\theta\) tiles \([0, 1]\) in the order \((T = 0, T = 1, T = 2, T = 3)\).
Consider instead the tiling in the order \((T = 0, T = 2, T = 1, T = 3)\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
V'_\theta(0, U) &= U(1-\theta)^3, \\
V'_\theta(2, U) &= (1-\theta)^3 + 3 U\,\theta^2(1-\theta), \\
V'_\theta(1, U) &= (1-\theta)^3 + 3\theta^2(1-\theta) + 3U\,\theta(1-\theta)^2, \\
V'_\theta(3, U) &= 1 - \theta^3(1 - U),
\end{aligned}
\]
each linear in \(U\) with slope \(p_\theta(t)\). The four image
intervals \([0, A_1], [A_1, A_2], [A_2, A_3], [A_3, 1]\) (with
\(A_1 = (1-\theta)^3\), \(A_2 = (1-\theta)(1 - 2\theta + 4\theta^2)\),
\(A_3 = 1 - \theta^3\)) partition \([0, 1]\) for every
\(\theta \in (0, 1)\).

\emph{Verification of (R1), (R2), (R3\(_U\)):}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item (R3\(_U\)): each \(V'_\theta(t, \cdot)\) is linear in \(U\) with
  positive slope \(p_\theta(t)\). \(\checkmark\)
\item (R2): the four image intervals tile \([0, 1]\); the joint mixture
  (sample \(X\) then \(U\)) has uniform density on each piece by the
  slope identity, hence is exactly \(U(0, 1)\). \(\checkmark\)
\item (R1): direct differentiation gives
  \(\partial_\theta V'_\theta(t, U)\) non-positive at every
  \((t, U) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\} \times [0, 1]\) and every
  \(\theta \in (0, 1)\). The binding constraints occur at the swap-piece
  endpoint \(A_2\), where
  \(\partial_\theta A_2 = -3(2\theta - 1)^2 \le 0\) --- a perfect square
  with a minus sign, the algebraic accident that lets the non-canonical
  ordering preserve (R1) for \(\mathrm{Bin}(3, \theta)\) where it fails
  for \(\mathrm{Bin}(2, \theta)\). The remaining endpoint and
  slope-driven terms in \(\partial_\theta V'_\theta(t, U)\) are linear
  in \(U\) with non-positive values at both endpoints \(U = 0, 1\), so
  non-positive throughout. \(\checkmark\)
\end{itemize}

\emph{(R4) is violated everywhere:}
\[
V'_\theta(1, U) - V'_\theta(2, U) \;=\; 3\theta^2(1-\theta) + 3U\,\theta(1-\theta)(1 - 2\theta).
\]
At \(U = 0\) this equals \(3\theta^2(1 - \theta) > 0\) for every
\(\theta \in (0, 1)\); the expression evaluates to
\(3\theta(1-\theta)^2 > 0\) at \(U = 1\); linearity in \(U\) (with both
endpoints strictly positive) gives \(V'_\theta(1, U) > V'_\theta(2, U)\)
for every \((\theta, U) \in (0, 1) \times [0, 1]\). Since
\(T(X = 1) < T(X = 2)\) but \(V'_\theta(1, U) > V'_\theta(2, U)\), (R4)
fails. So \(V^*_\theta\) and \(V'_\theta\) both satisfy (R1)--(R3\(_U\))
and both yield calibrated pivots via \(r = \Phi^{-1}(1 - V)\); they
differ only in the lex-ordering of \(T\)-values relative to \(U\), and
(R4) selects \(V^*_\theta\).

\textbf{Why (R4) is automatic in the continuous case.} In the continuous
case (\S\ref{subsec:5.2}, \S\ref{subsec:5.1}), \(V_\theta(X) = F_\theta(T(X))\)
is monotone non-decreasing in \(T(X)\) by definition of the CDF, so the
canonical pivot \(\tilde r(\theta; t) = \Phi^{-1}(1 - F_\theta(t))\) is
monotone non-increasing in \(t\) under increasing MLR --- (R4) at the
\(t\)-level holds automatically. (R4) becomes a non-redundant assumption
only in the discrete setting, where monotone rearrangement of an atomic
source onto a continuous target leaves a permutation freedom across the
\(T\)-atoms. For small atom counts (\(\mathrm{Bin}(1, \theta)\) and
\(\mathrm{Bin}(2, \theta)\)) the permutation freedom is itself
constrained out by (R1) + (R3\(_U\)); only at \(\mathrm{Bin}(3, \theta)\)
and beyond does a non-canonical permutation with all-monotone-in-\(\theta\)
endpoints become available (the perfect-square accident
\(-3(2\theta - 1)^2 \le 0\) above), requiring (R4) as an extra
condition.

\subsubsection{Mid-p alternative}\label{subsubsec:5.7.3}

A deterministic alternative is the ``mid-p'' CD, fixing \(U = 1/2\): \[
H^\mathrm{mid}_\theta(X) \;:=\; 1 - V_\theta(X, 1/2).
\] This is approximately calibrated: the law of \(V_\theta(X, 1/2)\)
is purely atomic, so its total variation from \(U(0,1)\) equals
\(1\); the relevant metric is the \emph{Kolmogorov (uniform CDF)
distance} \(K(\theta) := \sup_v |P_\theta(V^\mathrm{mid} \le v) - v|\),
which equals \(\tfrac{1}{2} \sup_{t \in \mathrm{supp}(T)} p_\theta(t)\)
(achieved at \(v = F_\theta(t_*) - \tfrac{1}{2} p_\theta(t_*)\) for the
mode \(t_*\)). For \(\mathrm{Bin}(n, \theta)\) at moderate \(\theta\),
the local CLT gives \(\sup_t p_\theta(t) = \Theta(1/\sqrt{n})\), so
\(K(\theta) = O(1/\sqrt{n})\). For applications where ease of
implementation outweighs the small calibration error, the mid-p variant
trains \(r(\theta; X)\) directly with \(U\) fixed at \(1/2\), dropping
the auxiliary input. See \citet{HwangYang2001} for the optimality theory
of mid-p in the closely related contingency-table setting.

\section*{Part IV — Multivariate generalization}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Part IV — Multivariate generalization}

In dimension \(d > 1\), the calibration constraint
\(r \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) admits the entire orthogonal group
\(O(d)\) --- any rotation of a calibrated pivot is again calibrated,
because the standard Gaussian is rotation-invariant. So the singleton
result of Part III cannot hold without additional architectural
restriction. We restrict to \emph{autoregressive triangular} flows,
where each coordinate \(r_k\) depends only on inputs with index
\(\le k\). Within that class, Theorem A-d again pins the calibration
manifold to a singleton --- the \emph{Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement}.
For Gaussian \(X \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma)\), the
rearrangement has the closed form \(L^{-1}(\theta - X)\) where
\(LL^T = \Sigma\) is the Cholesky factorization. The architecture
scales linearly in \(d\) (its Jacobian is lower-triangular).

\section{The triangular-autoregressive flow construction}\label{sec:6}

\paragraph{From 1D to multivariate, and why singleton uniqueness needs help.}
Theorems A and C of Part III pinned down the calibration manifold to a
singleton in 1D. In \(d > 1\), this is no longer automatic. The
calibration constraint \(r(\theta; X) \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\)
is invariant under the entire orthogonal group \(O(d)\) --- any rotation
of a calibrated \(r\) remains calibrated, because the standard Gaussian
is rotation-symmetric. (R1) coordinatewise eliminates the
\(\mathbb{Z}_2^d\) sign-flip subgroup but not the continuous rotation
group. So in \(d > 1\), without further architectural restriction, the
manifold is at least \(d(d-1)/2\)-dimensional; we will not have anything
like a unique answer.

The restriction we will impose is structural: each output coordinate
\(r_k\) depends only on input coordinates \(X_j\) and \(\theta_j\) with
\(j \le k\). A pivot of this form is called an \emph{autoregressive}
flow; its Jacobian matrices in \(\theta\) and in \(X\) are both
lower-triangular, the determinant is a product of \(d\) diagonal
entries, and the architecture is fast to train. The pay-off, which the
rest of \S\ref{sec:6} develops, is that within this autoregressive
class the calibration manifold collapses back to a singleton --- the
\emph{Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement} (\S\ref{subsec:6.3},
Theorem A-d). For Gaussian \(X \mid \theta\), the KR rearrangement has
the simple closed form \(L^{-1}(\theta - X)\) with \(LL^T = \Sigma\).
Standard references on the autoregressive-flow architecture include the
masked autoregressive flow of \citet{PapamakariosEtAl2017}, the survey
of \citet{PapamakariosEtAl2021}, and the neural spline flow of
\citet{DurkanEtAl2019}.

\subsection{The architectural class}\label{subsec:6.1}

Let
\(\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d) \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\)
and \(X \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) (or higher-dim \(X\)
with \(d\)-dim \(r\)). We work in the class of pivots of the form
\(r = (r_1, \ldots, r_d)\) where each component obeys:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{(R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) Autoregressive monotonicity in
  \(\theta\):} \emph{(dependence)} \(r_k\) is a measurable function of
  \((\theta_{\le k}, X)\) only; \emph{(monotonicity)} at fixed
  \(\theta_{<k}, X\), the map \(\theta_k \mapsto r_k\) is strictly
  increasing.
\item
  \textbf{(R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) Autoregressive monotonicity in \(X\):}
  \emph{(dependence)} \(r_k\) is a measurable function of
  \((\theta, X_{\le k})\) only; \emph{(monotonicity)} at fixed
  \(\theta, X_{<k}\), the map \(X_k \mapsto r_k\) is strictly monotone
  with a consistent sign across coordinates (we use the convention
  \(\partial_{X_k} r_k < 0\), matching the KR map
  \(r_k^{\mathrm{KR}} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - F_k^{(\theta)}(X_k \mid X_{<k}))\)).
\end{itemize}

Both clauses of (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) are load-bearing: the
\emph{dependence} clause is what makes \(\partial_X r\) lower-triangular
(\S\ref{subsec:6.2}) and \(\sigma(\theta, X_{<k}) = \sigma(\theta, r_{<k})\)
(used in the induction of \S\ref{subsec:6.3}); the \emph{monotonicity}
clause is what makes the conditional pushforward of \(X_k\) under
\(r_k\) a measure-preserving rearrangement onto \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\),
the input to the Lemma~4.2 rigidity argument.

(R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) and (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) are the autoregressive
specializations of (R1) and (R2). (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) implies the
coordinate-wise version of (R1) by inspection; (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\))
implies (R2) (a lower-triangular Jacobian with strictly monotone
diagonal entries has full rank, so \(r\) is a local diffeomorphism in
\(X\) at each \(\theta\)). The strict-propriety argument of
\S\ref{subsec:3.2} therefore applies verbatim in the autoregressive
class, with no separate derivation needed.

This class admits at least two practical parameterizations:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Additive form (Gaussian-like models, \S\ref{subsec:8.1}--8.3):}
  \[r_k(\theta, X) = a_k(\theta_k;\, \theta_{<k}, X_{<k}) \;-\; b_k(X_k;\, \theta_{<k}, X_{<k}),\]
  with \(a_k\) a UMNN monotone in \(\theta_k\) and \(b_k\) a UMNN
  monotone in \(X_k\), both conditioned on the autoregressive context.
\item
  \textbf{Doubly-monotone UMNN form (non-additive interactions, \S\ref{subsec:8.4}):}
  \[r_k(\theta, X) = b_k(X_k;\, X_{<k}) \;+\; \int_{\theta_{k,\mathrm{ref}}}^{\theta_k} \mathrm{softplus}\bigl(\alpha_k(t;\, \theta_{<k}, X_{<k}) + \beta_k(X_k;\, X_{<k})\bigr)\, dt,\]
  monotone in \(\theta_k\) by positivity of the softplus integrand
  (giving R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)). For monotonicity in \(X_k\) (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)),
  we further require that \(b_k\) is a UMNN in \(X_k\) and that
  \(\beta_k\) is monotone non-decreasing in \(X_k\) (also implemented as
  a UMNN, or constant in \(X_k\)). Then
  \(\partial_{X_k} r_k = \partial_{X_k} b_k + \int \mathrm{softplus}'(\cdot)\,\partial_{X_k}\beta_k\, dt\)
  is a sum of non-negative terms with the first strictly positive, so
  monotonicity holds.
\end{enumerate}

Both forms satisfy (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) and (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\));
the Knothe--Rosenblatt uniqueness theorem below applies to both.

\paragraph{A concrete \(d = 2\) example.}
To anchor the abstract definitions, take \(d = 2\), additive form,
\(X \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, I_2)\). A specific
autoregressive triangular flow in this setting is
\[
r_1(\theta_1, X_1) \;=\; \alpha_a \cdot a(\theta_1) \;-\; \alpha_b \cdot b(X_1),
\qquad
r_2(\theta, X) \;=\; a_2(\theta_2;\, \theta_1, X_1) \;-\; b_2(X_2;\, \theta_1, X_1),
\]
where \(a, b, a_2, b_2\) are scalar UMNNs and \(\alpha_a, \alpha_b > 0\)
are learnable scalars (initialized to give effective slope away from
\(1\) so optimization has to find the right scale; see \S\ref{subsec:7.1}).
The first coordinate \(r_1\) is a 1D additive flow in
\((\theta_1, X_1)\) only --- no \(\theta_2, X_2\) dependence by
(R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) and (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)). The second
coordinate \(r_2\) is itself a 1D additive flow in
\((\theta_2, X_2)\), but with the \((\theta_1, X_1)\) values fed as
context to the conditioning networks \(a_2\) and \(b_2\). The whole map
is monotone-in-\(\theta\) and monotone-in-\(X\) coordinatewise, in the
autoregressive sense; the Jacobians in \(\theta\) and in \(X\) are
both \(2 \times 2\) lower-triangular. This is exactly the architecture
used in the \S\ref{subsec:8.2} (\(\Sigma = I_2\)) and \S\ref{subsec:8.3}
(\(\Sigma\) correlated) experiments (Fig.~\ref{fig:c5}).

\subsection{Jacobian structure and computational properties}\label{subsec:6.2}

In either parameterization, both Jacobians are lower-triangular by
construction: \[
\bigl[\partial_\theta r\bigr]_{k\ell} = \begin{cases} \partial_{\theta_k} r_k > 0 & k = \ell \\ \text{(free)} & \ell < k \\ 0 & \ell > k \end{cases}
\qquad
\bigl[\partial_X r\bigr]_{k\ell} = \begin{cases} \partial_{X_k} r_k < 0 & k = \ell \\ \text{(free)} & \ell < k \\ 0 & \ell > k \end{cases}
\] (sign convention on the \(X\)-diagonal: negative throughout, matching
\(\partial_{X_k} r_k^{\mathrm{KR}} = -1/\sigma_k(\theta, X_{<k}) < 0\)).
The determinant is the product of diagonals, and \[
\log\bigl|\det \partial_X r\bigr| \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{d} \log \bigl|\partial_{X_k} r_k\bigr|.
\] For the \emph{additive} parameterization, \(\partial_{X_k} r_k = -\partial_{X_k} b_k\),
closed-form from the UMNN's softplus integrand. For the
\emph{doubly-monotone} parameterization, \(\partial_{X_k} r_k\) involves
the integral \(\int \mathrm{softplus}'\,\partial_{X_k}\beta_k\,dt\),
which requires the same Gauss--Legendre quadrature already used for the
\(\theta\)-integral evaluating \(r_k\). In both cases, no
autograd-on-autograd is needed; this is essential for stable training in
\(d > 1\).

\textbf{Connected CD level sets.} Under (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) and
\(C^1\) regularity, the sub-level set
\(\{\theta : \|r(\theta; X)\|^2 \le c\}\) is the image of a continuous
autoregressive interval-valued selection (each
\(\theta_k\)-slice at fixed \(\theta_{<k}\) is an interval by strict
monotonicity, with endpoints continuous in \(\theta_{<k}\) by \(C^1\)
regularity), hence connected as a subset of \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Confidence
sets via the chi-square inversion are therefore well-defined geometric
objects.

\textbf{Linear scaling in \(d\).} All operations cost \(O(d)\) per
sample, vs.~\(O(d^3)\) for a general dense Jacobian.

\subsection{Multivariate uniqueness: Knothe--Rosenblatt}\label{subsec:6.3}

\paragraph{What we are about to prove.}
The intro to \S\ref{sec:6} explained why the multivariate calibration
manifold cannot be a singleton without architectural restriction. This
subsection shows that the autoregressive triangular restriction
(R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) + (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) is enough to collapse
the manifold back to a singleton. The singleton has a name from
optimal-transport theory: the Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement. The
strategy is induction on the coordinate index --- the base case (\(k = 1\))
is just Theorem A applied at fixed \(\theta_1\), and the inductive step
identifies \(r_k\) by applying the general-source form of Lemma~4.2
(\S\ref{subsec:4.2} remark) to the conditional law
\(X_k \mid \theta, X_{<k}\).

\emph{Optimal-transport context, briefly.} Between two probability
measures on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the \textbf{Knothe--Rosenblatt
rearrangement} \citep{Knothe1957, Rosenblatt1952} is the unique transport
map of triangular form: each coordinate transformation conditions on
previously-transformed coordinates. It differs from the \textbf{Brenier
map} \citep{Brenier1991}, which is the unique transport map that is the
gradient of a convex function (the \(L^2\)-optimal transport plan). KR
depends on coordinate ordering; Brenier does not. Both are bijections
between the two measures; KR is the natural fit for autoregressive
architectures because its triangular structure aligns with the network's
autoregressive masking. For our purposes, the relevant fact is that the
Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement between
\(\mathrm{Law}(X \mid \theta)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) is given
coordinatewise by \(r_k^{\mathrm{KR}} = \Phi^{-1}\!\bigl(1 - F_k^{(\theta)}(X_k \mid X_{<k})\bigr)\),
which is the formal statement below.

\textbf{Theorem A-d (Multivariate uniqueness within the autoregressive
triangular class).} \emph{Let
\(X \mid \theta \sim p(\cdot \mid \theta)\) be a continuous model with
the property that, for each \(k\), the conditional CDF
\(F_k^{(\theta)}(\cdot \mid X_{<k}) := P(X_k \leq \cdot \mid \theta, X_{<k})\)
is \(C^1\) and strictly increasing on the support of
\(X_k \mid \theta, X_{<k}\). Within the class of autoregressive
triangular pivots satisfying (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) and
(R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) with each \(r_k \in C^1\) in its
\(X_k\)-argument, the calibration manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) consists of a
unique function --- the \textbf{Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement}:} \[
r_k^\mathrm{KR}(\theta, X) = \Phi^{-1}\bigl(1 - F_k^{(\theta)}(X_k \mid X_{<k})\bigr).
\]

\emph{Proof.} Induct on \(k\).

\emph{Base case (\(k = 1\)).} Under (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)),
\(r_1: \Theta \times \mathcal{X}_1 \to \mathbb{R}\) depends on \(X\)
only through \(X_1\). It may depend on \(\theta\) generally, but by
(R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) only through \(\theta_1\). The full calibration
constraint requires
\(r_1(\theta, X_1) \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Apply Theorem A
in \(X_1\)-coordinates at each fixed \(\theta_1\) (with other
\(\theta\)-components irrelevant by (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\))): the unique
monotone-in-\(X_1\) pushforward of the marginal \(X_1 \mid \theta_1\) to
\(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) is \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - F_1^{(\theta_1)}(X_1))\).
(R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) fixes the sign.

\emph{Inductive step (\(k > 1\)).} Assume
\(r_1, \ldots, r_{k-1}\) are uniquely identified (so each \(r_j\) for
\(j < k\) is the KR rearrangement of its level). Consider
\(r_k(\theta, X_{\le k})\) at fixed \((\theta, X_{<k})\).

\emph{\(\sigma\)-algebra equivalence.} By (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) applied
at each level \(j < k\), the map \(X_j \mapsto r_j(\theta, X_{\le j})\)
is a strictly monotone bijection at fixed \((\theta, X_{<j})\).
Composing autoregressively, the map
\((\theta, X_{<k}) \mapsto (\theta, r_{<k})\) is a triangular bijection,
so \(\sigma(\theta, X_{<k}) = \sigma(\theta, r_{<k})\). Conditioning on
\(r_{<k}\) given \(\theta\) is therefore equivalent to conditioning on
\(X_{<k}\) given \(\theta\).

\emph{Calibration $\Rightarrow$ conditional Gaussianity.} The full
calibration constraint \(r(\theta; X) \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\)
makes \(r_1, \ldots, r_d \mid \theta\) jointly standard normal, with
independent coordinates (\(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) has diagonal covariance,
so the marginals are independent). Independence + standard-normal
marginals means each \(r_k\) is \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) regardless of what
we condition on among the other coordinates. Combined with the
\(\sigma\)-algebra identity above, this gives
\(r_k \mid \theta, X_{<k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

\emph{1D rigidity.} By (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)),
\(X_k \mapsto r_k(\theta, X_{\le k})\) is strictly monotone at fixed
\((\theta, X_{<k})\). Apply the general-source form of Lemma~4.2
(\S\ref{subsec:4.2} remark) with source the conditional density
\(F_k^{(\theta)}(\cdot \mid X_{<k})\) (continuous, positive on its
support by hypothesis) and target \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\): the unique
monotone-in-\(X_k\) pushforward is
\(\Phi^{-1}(1 - F_k^{(\theta)}(X_k \mid X_{<k}))\) for the
direction-decreasing branch, or its negative for the
direction-increasing branch. (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) fixes the sign of
the \(\theta_k\)-dependence: the decreasing-in-\(X_k\),
increasing-in-\(\theta_k\) branch is the one consistent with the sign
conventions of (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) and (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)), giving
\(r_k = r_k^{\mathrm{KR}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

\paragraph{The Gaussian case: a worked example.}
Theorem A-d identifies \(r^{\mathrm{KR}}\) in terms of conditional
CDFs. For the Gaussian model \(X \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma)\),
that abstract description simplifies dramatically. Take the 2D
correlated case used in the \S\ref{subsec:8.3} experiment:
\[
\Sigma \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The Cholesky factorization \(LL^T = \Sigma\) gives
\(L = \bigl(\!\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & \sqrt{0.75}\end{smallmatrix}\!\bigr)\)
and consequently
\(L^{-1} = \bigl(\!\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -0.577 & 1.155\end{smallmatrix}\!\bigr)\).
The KR pivot in this case is just \(L^{-1}(\theta - X)\):
\[
r_1^{\mathrm{KR}}(\theta, X) \;=\; \theta_1 - X_1,
\qquad
r_2^{\mathrm{KR}}(\theta, X) \;=\; 1.155(\theta_2 - X_2) \;-\; 0.577(\theta_1 - X_1).
\]
The first coordinate is just the 1D location-normal pivot of
\S\ref{subsec:1.2}: \(\theta_1\) minus \(X_1\), no cross-coupling.
The second coordinate is the same idea (\(\theta_2\) minus \(X_2\)),
but with a cross-coupling correction term that depends on the first-
coordinate residual. The strength of the cross-coupling is set by the
off-diagonal of \(L^{-1}\) (i.e.\ by \(\Sigma\)'s correlation
structure). This generalizes to arbitrary \(d\):

\textbf{Corollary (multivariate location-Gaussian).} \emph{For
\(X \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, \Sigma)\) with \(\Sigma\)
positive definite, let \(LL^T = \Sigma\) be the \textbf{Cholesky
factorization} (\(L\) lower-triangular with positive diagonal). Then the
KR pivot is} \[
r^{\mathrm{KR}}(\theta, X) = L^{-1}(\theta - X).
\]

\emph{Proof.} The cleanest route bypasses the Cholesky-regression
algebra. By definition of the Cholesky factor, \(Z := L^{-1}(X - \theta)\)
satisfies \(Z \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\), so
\(-Z = L^{-1}(\theta - X) \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) as well
(the standard Gaussian is sign-symmetric coordinatewise). The map
\((\theta, X) \mapsto L^{-1}(\theta - X)\) is
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item lower-triangular in both \(\theta\) and \(X\) (since \(L^{-1}\) is
  lower-triangular, with positive diagonal \(1/L_{kk}\)),
\item strictly increasing in \(\theta_k\) at each fixed
  \(\theta_{<k}, X\) (diagonal of \(L^{-1}\) is positive), so it
  satisfies (R1\(^\mathrm{auto}\)),
\item strictly decreasing in \(X_k\) at each fixed \(\theta, X_{<k}\)
  (with negative \(X_k\)-diagonal \(-1/L_{kk}\)), so it satisfies
  (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) with the sign convention of \S\ref{subsec:6.1}.
\end{itemize}
\(L^{-1}(\theta - X)\) is thus a member of the autoregressive
calibration manifold. By Theorem A-d's uniqueness, it equals
\(r^{\mathrm{KR}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

\textbf{Remarks.}

\emph{1. KR depends on coordinate ordering.} Different orderings of the
coordinates of \(X\) (and correspondingly of \(\theta\) at each
autoregressive level) yield different KR pivots, all valid CD pivots,
all in their respective calibration manifolds (provided that for each
ordering the conditional CDFs \(F_{\pi(k)}^{(\theta)}(\cdot \mid X_{\pi(<k)})\)
satisfy the \(C^1\)-strictly-increasing hypothesis of Theorem A-d ---
automatic for full-support continuous models like multivariate Gaussian).
The architectural choice of autoregressive order in the network
implicitly selects one. For models with a natural ordering (time series;
spatial data with a fixed sweep), use the data-defined order; for
unordered data, any ordering is correct, with different choices giving
different but inferentially equivalent pivots.

\emph{2. If the calibration manifold is a singleton, why do we see
pointwise residuals in §\ref{subsec:8.3}?} A natural objection: the
manifold is supposedly a unique function, but the experiments in
\S\ref{subsec:8.3} report residuals between the trained \(r_k\) and the
canonical \(r_k^{\mathrm{KR}}\) at individual \((\theta, X)\) points,
on the order of a few percent of the Jacobian magnitude. Where do those
residuals come from?

Two sources, neither of which contradicts the singleton result. First,
the theorem identifies the \emph{function} \(r\) up to the calibration
manifold's only ambiguity (sign + autoregressive ordering, both of
which are fixed by (R1) and the architectural choice). But the
\emph{architectural decomposition} \(r_k = a_k - b_k\) in the additive
form is \emph{not} uniquely determined: the simultaneous shift
\(a_k \mapsto a_k + h(\theta_{<k}, X_{<k})\),
\(b_k \mapsto b_k + h(\theta_{<k}, X_{<k})\) by any context-dependent
function \(h\) leaves \(r_k = a_k - b_k\) unchanged and preserves the
UMNN-monotonicity of each component (since \(h\) is constant in the
primary argument of both networks, it can be absorbed into the UMNN
bias). Only the difference \(r_k = a_k - b_k\) is identified, so a
finite-network trained instance can land anywhere on this gauge orbit.
Second, training is finite: at \(\sim 5000\) samples, the trained \(r\)
is a finite-network approximation of \(r^{\mathrm{KR}}\), with residual
variance scaling like \(N^{-1}\). The residuals in \S\ref{subsec:8.3}
are dominated by these two effects, not by non-uniqueness of the
population-level optimum.

\emph{3. The role of (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) in multivariate.} Both
clauses of (R2\(^\mathrm{auto}\)) (\S\ref{subsec:6.1}) are needed in the
induction, but for distinct reasons.
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item Without the \emph{dependence} clause, \(\partial_X r\) fails to be
  lower-triangular (the Jacobian formula C-6.2-tri-jac breaks) and the
  \(\sigma\)-algebra identity \(\sigma(\theta, X_{<k}) = \sigma(\theta, r_{<k})\)
  used in the inductive step also fails.
\item Without the \emph{monotonicity} clause, Lemma~4.2's preimage-density
  contradiction at a critical point of \(X_k \mapsto r_k\) no longer
  rules out non-monotone \(r_k\); the conditional calibration constraint
  \(r_k \mid \theta, X_{<k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) can then be satisfied
  by multiple distinct \(r_k\). This is the multivariate analogue of the
  autograd-Jacobian failure mode of \S\ref{subsec:8.4}.
\end{itemize}
The same architectural prescription that fixes the 1D non-additive case
is what makes the inductive proof go through in \(d > 1\).

\subsection{Inference: direct pivot inversion}\label{subsec:6.4}

At an observed \(X_\mathrm{obs}\), the level-\(\alpha\) confidence set
is \[
\mathcal{C}_\alpha(X_\mathrm{obs}) := \bigl\{\theta : \|r(\theta; X_\mathrm{obs})\|^2 \leq \chi^2_{d, \alpha}\bigr\}.
\] Under (C1), \(\|r(\theta_0; X)\|^2 \mid \theta_0 \sim \chi^2_d\) for
every \(\theta_0 \in \Theta\) on the inferential region, so
\(\mathcal{C}_\alpha\) has exact frequentist coverage: \[
\mathbb{P}_{X \sim P_{\theta_0}}\bigl(\theta_0 \in \mathcal{C}_\alpha(X)\bigr) = \alpha.
\] The set \(\mathcal{C}_\alpha\) is found by numerical root-finding in
\(\theta\): since \(r\) is monotone in \(\theta\) (autoregressively),
each ``slice'' \(\theta_k \mid \theta_{<k}\) is a 1D root-finding
problem.

\paragraph{From \(\rho\)-a.e.\ calibration to pointwise calibration.}
The KL identity in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:strictprop} concludes
that any NF-MLE minimizer is calibrated for \(\rho\)-a.e.\ \(\theta_0\).
Coverage at \emph{every} \(\theta_0\) inside the inferential region is
a strictly stronger property, so closing the gap is load-bearing. The
lift uses two ingredients. First, every architecture in this paper
makes \(r\) continuous (in fact, smooth) in \(\theta\) by construction
--- UMNN integrals and triangular flows are both \(C^1\) in the
parameter argument. Second, \(\rho\) has full support on the
inferential region by the standing assumption of \S\ref{subsec:2.1}, so
the set on which calibration holds is dense.

The continuity-plus-dense-support argument is then short. Fix a target
\(\theta_0\) inside the inferential region. The map
\(\theta \mapsto \mathrm{Law}(r(\theta; X) \mid \theta_0 \text{ if we substitute } \theta = \theta_0)\)
is continuous in \(\theta\) in the weak-convergence sense, because
\(r\) is continuous in \(\theta\) and the law of \(X \mid \theta_0\) is
fixed. There exists a sequence
\(\theta^{(n)} \to \theta_0\) along which calibration holds (the
\(\rho\)-a.e.\ set is dense in the inferential region). The weak limit
of the laws \(\mathrm{Law}(r(\theta^{(n)}; X) \mid \theta_0)\) is
\(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) (the calibrated law along the sequence) and
also \(\mathrm{Law}(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0)\) (by continuity).
Uniqueness of weak limits gives
\(r(\theta_0; X) \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) at the
particular \(\theta_0\) chosen, hence at every \(\theta_0\) in the
inferential region.

\textbf{Worked example (1D location-normal).} With
\(r^*(\theta; X) = \theta - X\),
\(\|r^*(\theta; X_\mathrm{obs})\|^2 = (\theta - X_\mathrm{obs})^2 \le \chi^2_{1, \alpha} = z_\alpha^2\)
gives
\(\mathcal{C}_\alpha = [X_\mathrm{obs} - z_\alpha, X_\mathrm{obs} + z_\alpha]\)
--- the standard \(z\)-interval, as expected.

\section{Architecture and training}\label{sec:7}

\subsection{UMNN building blocks}\label{subsec:7.1}

\paragraph{The idea, in one sentence.}
Take any strictly positive function on \(\mathbb{R}\), integrate it
from a reference point to \(z\), and the result is a strictly
monotone-increasing function of \(z\). This is the fundamental theorem
of calculus: a positive integrand gives a strictly increasing
antiderivative. An \emph{Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Network}
(UMNN; \citealp{WehenkelLouppe2019}) is just that construction with a
neural network playing the role of the positive function: the neural
network itself is unconstrained (any architecture, any signs), but
its strictly-positive composition (via \(\sigma_+\) below) makes the
overall integrated function monotone by construction. The Jacobian of
the deployed function is closed-form (it is the positive integrand
itself), so no recursive autograd is needed at training time --- which
is the reason UMNN is the right tool for enforcing (R2)
architecturally rather than by penalty.

\textbf{Definition (UMNN; \citealp{WehenkelLouppe2019}, with two
implementation substitutions).} A UMNN parameterizes a scalar function
\(g(z; c)\) that is strictly monotone-increasing in \(z\) at fixed
context \(c\) by writing \[
g(z; c) = \mathrm{bias}(c) + \int_{z_0}^z \sigma_+\bigl(\mathrm{MLP}(t, c)\bigr)\, dt,
\] where \(\mathrm{MLP}(\cdot, c)\) is an unconstrained neural network
and \(\sigma_+\) is any strictly positive activation. The original
formulation \citep{WehenkelLouppe2019} uses
\(\sigma_+(x) = \mathrm{ELU}(x) + 1\) with Clenshaw--Curtis quadrature;
in this paper we substitute \(\sigma_+ = \mathrm{softplus}\) with
\(n = 12\)-node Gauss--Legendre quadrature, which is more standard in
modern UMNN implementations and which simplifies the closed-form
derivative \(\partial_z g(z; c) = \sigma_+(\mathrm{MLP}(z, c))\) used
as the Jacobian factor in NF-MLE without recursive autograd. The deployed \(g\) under quadrature is a positive
linear combination of \(\sigma_+\) values evaluated at the nodes, so it
inherits strict monotonicity from the integrand; we use the analytical
\(\sigma_+(\mathrm{MLP}(z, c))\) as the Jacobian factor rather than the
exact derivative of the quadrature polynomial, a discrepancy that is
negligible at \(n = 12\) on the smooth \(\sigma_+ \circ \mathrm{MLP}\)
integrand class used here.

Hidden width 32 throughout. Final-layer weights initialized to zero so
the bias dominates at init and the model starts near a near-identity
transformation. We multiply the integrated function by a learnable
positive scalar \(\alpha\) (initialized at \(0.5 / \ln 2 \approx 0.72\),
giving init effective slope \(0.5\), deliberately wrong) so optimization
has to find the right scale.

\subsection{Diagnostics}\label{subsec:7.3}

For each trained model:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Pivot RMSE vs \(r^*\)} on a dense grid, where \(r^*\) is
  closed-form. Sanity check that the model recovered the analytical
  answer.
\item
  \textbf{Marginal PIT.} \(\Phi(r_k(\theta_i, X_i))\) on
  training-distribution samples should be \(U(0,1)\).
  Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) test.
\item
  \textbf{Conditional PIT.} At fixed \(\theta_0\),
  \(r_k(\theta_0, X) \mid \theta_0\) should be \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). KS
  test at \(\geq 5\) \(\theta_0\) values.
\item
  \textbf{Joint Mahalanobis (\(d > 1\)).}
  \(\|r(\theta_0, X)\|^2 \mid \theta_0\) should be \(\chi^2_d\). KS
  test.
\item
  \textbf{Coverage.}
  \(\mathbb{P}_{X \sim P_{\theta_0}}(\theta_0 \in \mathcal{C}_\alpha(X))\)
  across \(\theta_0\) and confidence levels
  \(\alpha \in \{0.5, 0.68, 0.9, 0.95\}\). This is the inferentially
  relevant test.
\end{enumerate}

Diagnostic 5 is inferentially primary --- it is the property the
framework promises and the property practitioners rely on. Diagnostics
1--4 are interpretability checks that help diagnose \emph{where} a
calibration failure lives. Diagnostic~2 (marginal PIT) is implied by
Diagnostic~3 (conditional PIT) in the population, since marginalizing
out \(\theta\) preserves uniformity; we report both because they have
complementary finite-sample power. Diagnostic~4 (joint Mahalanobis) is
\emph{not} implied by component-wise Diagnostic~3 in \(d > 1\): each
\(r_k \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) does not entail
\(r \mid \theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) since the components could
be correlated --- the Hermans-type failure pattern.

sensitive to:

\textbf{Statistical noise floor for KS.} Under the null,
\(\sqrt{N} \cdot \mathrm{KS}\) has the Kolmogorov limiting distribution
\citep[\S14.2]{LehmannRomano2005} with
\(\Pr(\sqrt{N}\cdot\mathrm{KS} > 1.628) = 0.01\). The relevant
sample size depends on which statistic is being evaluated.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item \emph{Marginal} statistics (Diagnostic~2; the joint Mahalanobis
  test of Diagnostic~4 if averaged over \(\theta\)) use the full sample
  \(N\). At \(N = 5000\) the floor is
  \(1.628/\sqrt{5000} \approx 0.023\); at \(N = 6000\), \(\approx 0.021\).
\item \emph{Per-conditional} statistics (Diagnostic~3 evaluated at a
  fixed \(\theta_0\); per-\(\theta_0\) joint Mahalanobis) use the
  per-bin sample size \(N/k\) where \(k\) is the number of evaluation
  bins. At \(N = 5000\), \(k = 5\) bins gives a per-bin floor of
  \(1.628/\sqrt{1000} \approx 0.052\); at \(N = 6000\), \(k = 5\) bins
  gives \(\approx 0.047\); at \(N = 5000\), \(k = 7\) bins gives
  \(\approx 0.061\).
\end{itemize}

When we interpret a conditional-KS value as ``borderline'' in
\S\ref{sec:8}, the relevant comparison is to the per-bin floor, not the
marginal one. The original v6 commentary in \S\ref{subsec:8.2} and
\S\ref{subsec:8.4} used the marginal floor against conditional
statistics, which is the wrong comparison; against the per-bin floor,
the same values are clean. The corrected comparisons appear inline at
each experiment.

Related diagnostic frameworks include simulation-based calibration (SBC;
\citealp{TaltsEtAl2018}), sampling-based accuracy testing (TARP;
\citealp{LemosEtAl2023}), and the LF2I conditional-coverage diagnostic
\citep{DalmassoEtAl2024}. Our coverage diagnostic (item 5) is closest
in spirit to the LF2I local-coverage check.

\section{Four experiments}\label{sec:8}

\paragraph{The structure of the empirical program.}
Each experiment in this section is targeted: it picks a problem where
the canonical pivot \(r^*\) is closed-form, trains the CD-SBI machinery
on it, and asks how close the trained \(r\) gets to \(r^*\) under each
of the §\ref{subsec:7.3} diagnostics. The four experiments together
cover the four theorem-statements of Parts III--IV: §\ref{subsec:8.1}
(1D location-normal) probes Theorem A, §\ref{subsec:8.2} (2D location
with \(\Sigma = I\)) probes the triangular-flow construction of
§\ref{subsec:6.1}--§\ref{subsec:6.2}, §\ref{subsec:8.3} (2D location
with correlated \(\Sigma\)) probes Theorem A-d via the Cholesky
corollary, and §\ref{subsec:8.4} (exponential rate) probes the
doubly-monotone architecture for non-additive pivots --- plus a
controlled ablation in §\ref{subsec:8.4} that deliberately violates
(R2) to demonstrate the failure mode of §\ref{subsec:3.5}. The
experiments are not validation in the sense of ``checking that the
method works'' --- the theory already establishes correctness in the
population limit. They validate that (i) finite-sample optimization
actually reaches the population optimum at practical training budgets,
and (ii) the architectural prescriptions matter as the theory says they
should.

prescription is expected to be even more load-bearing.

\subsection{Exponential rate: validating the non-additive case}\label{subsec:8.4}

\textbf{Setup.} \(X_i \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\theta)\) iid, \(n = 5\) samples
summed into \(T = \sum_i X_i\). \(\theta \sim U[0.3, 3.0]\). Truth
\(r^*(\theta, T) = \Phi^{-1}(F_{\chi^2_{10}}(2\theta T))\) ---
non-additive, with multiplicative \(\theta T\) interaction. This tests
whether the framework handles models outside the additive class of
\S\ref{subsec:8.1}--8.3.

\textbf{Doubly-monotone architecture (\S\ref{subsec:6.1}, form 2).} Both
monotonicities enforced architecturally, with \(X\)-dependence through
the sufficient statistic \(T\): \[
r(\theta, T) = b_\mathrm{umnn}(T) + \int_{\theta_\mathrm{ref}}^{\theta} \mathrm{softplus}\bigl(\alpha(t) + \beta_\mathrm{umnn}(T)\bigr)\, dt,
\] where \(b_\mathrm{umnn}, \beta_\mathrm{umnn}\) are
monotone-increasing UMNNs of \(T\), and \(\theta_\mathrm{ref}\) is taken
to be the center of the proposal support (which bounds the integral
domain symmetrically; the original draft used the lower endpoint, but
the centered choice gives the Gauss--Legendre quadrature better
conditioning). Then \(\partial_\theta r = \mathrm{softplus}(\cdot) > 0\)
(R1) and
\(\partial_T r = b'_\mathrm{umnn}(T) + \beta'_\mathrm{umnn}(T) \int_{\theta_\mathrm{ref}}^\theta \sigma(\cdot)\, dt > 0\)
(R2; both summands non-negative with \(b'_\mathrm{umnn} > 0\) strict),
both in closed form via the same quadrature used for \(r\) itself.

set (\(N = 6000\) draws for marginal PIT and pivot RMSE,

\(\theta_0\) values \(\times\) 50 \(X \mid \theta\) samples per nominal
coverage level). \(d = 1\) here, so \(\mathtt{JointMahalanobis}\) and
\(\mathtt{JacobianRecovery}\) (the \S\ref{subsec:8.2} and \S\ref{subsec:8.3}

a stylistic preference, for NF-MLE.

ablation result --- under-convergence at the standard recipe,
catastrophic folding at the longer recipe --- empirically confirms
that the architectural enforcement of (R2) is not a stylistic choice

\section*{Part VI — Practice and position}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Part VI — Practice and position}

Parts I--V develop the framework and validate it on small problems.
Part VI translates that work into practitioner guidance and situates
it in the SBI literature. Section~\ref{sec:9} gives a six-step recipe
for applying CD-SBI to a new model: identify the structure of the
truth, pick an architecture, enforce both monotonicities, train,
validate, invert. Section~\ref{sec:10} positions CD-SBI against
NPE/NLE/NRE and the frequentist-coverage SBI line (LF2I, WALDO, Box CD),
arguing that CD-SBI is the natural single-stage analogue: same NF-MLE
loss as SNL, same target as LF2I/WALDO (confidence sets), with neither
the two-stage critical-values branch nor the missing-coverage gap.

\section{Implementation recipe}\label{sec:9}

\paragraph{The architectural recipe, briefly.}
The previous Parts develop a single prescription for what a CD-SBI
implementation should look like: train a neural pivot \(r(\theta; X)\)
to minimize the NF-MLE loss
\(L(r) = \mathbb{E}_{(\theta, X)}\bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\|r\|^2 - \log|\det \partial r / \partial X|\bigr]\),
with the architecture chosen so that \(r\) is strictly monotone in
\(\theta\) (R1) and strictly monotone in \(X\) (R2) by construction ---
not by penalty, and not as an artifact of training. In 1D the
architecture is a UMNN-based additive or doubly-monotone flow
(§\ref{subsec:7.1}); in \(d > 1\) it is an autoregressive triangular
flow (§\ref{subsec:6.1}). The output is a calibrated CD pivot whose
chi-square inversion gives confidence sets with exact pointwise
coverage (§\ref{subsec:6.4}). What follows is the practitioner-facing
checklist.

A practitioner's checklist for applying CD-SBI to a new model:

in \((\theta, X)\)? Multiplicative? Does the sufficient statistic factor
through? This tells you which architecture to use:

\textbf{2. Enforce both monotonicities architecturally.} Never rely on
autograd-only Jacobians. The theoretical argument in \S\ref{subsec:3.5}
gives the mechanism (folding makes \(\hat p_r\) non-normalized and the
loss unbounded below); the \S\ref{subsec:8.4} ablation confirms it
empirically in one setup. Theory is doing the heavy lifting; the
single ablation is the smoking gun, not the entire basis. We treat
this as a hard rule because the failure mode is structural.

\textbf{3. Use a proposal \(\rho\) with full support on the region of
interest.} The framework is robust to the choice of \(\rho\) in the
population limit, but finite-sample performance benefits from \(\rho\)
covering the region where inference will be performed. Edge effects
(\S\ref{subsec:8.1}) appear near the support boundary.

\textbf{4. Train with NF-MLE.} No annealing schedule needed. Batch size
256--1024; Adam at \(\sim 3 \times 10^{-3}\); 3,000--10,000 steps
depending on problem complexity.

\textbf{5. Validate.} Run all five diagnostics in \S\ref{subsec:7.3}. The critical one
is coverage: if coverage matches nominal at multiple \(\theta_0\) and
multiple \(\alpha\) levels, the pivot is valid. If only marginal PIT
passes but conditional fails, you have the kind of conditional
miscalibration that \citet{HermansEtAl2022} documented in standard SBI
methods, and you need to revisit the architecture.

\textbf{6. At test time, invert.} Given observed \(X_\mathrm{obs}\),
compute
\(\mathcal{C}_\alpha = \{\theta : \|r(\theta, X_\mathrm{obs})\|^2 \le \chi^2_{d, \alpha}\}\)
by 1D root-finding (autoregressively, in \(d > 1\)).

\paragraph{What CD-SBI buys you, by comparison.}
The closest cousins of CD-SBI are the SBI methods that target
confidence sets (LF2I and WALDO) and the method with the same training
loss (SNL).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Versus LF2I and WALDO.} Same target (confidence sets with
  frequentist coverage), substantially different architecture. LF2I and
  WALDO are two-stage: a learned test statistic \(T(X; \theta)\) plus a
  separately-trained critical-values function \(c_\alpha(\theta)\) that
  is fit by quantile regression on a hold-out simulation set. CD-SBI is
  single-stage: the same forward pass through the trained network gives
  the CD and the confidence set, with the calibration enforced in the
  population limit by the architecture + loss combination rather than
  by a separate calibration sample. Lower inference compute at test
  time, fewer hyperparameters, but the architectural prescription is
  more restrictive (you commit to autoregressive triangular flows
  rather than any test statistic).
\item
  \textbf{Versus SNL.} Same NF-MLE training loss (Theorem 3.2 covers
  both), substantially different output. SNL trains a generic
  normalizing flow on \(X \mid \theta\) and uses MCMC over the learned
  surrogate likelihood combined with a user-supplied prior to extract
  posterior samples. CD-SBI trains a monotone-in-\(\theta\),
  monotone-in-\(X\) flow on the same data and outputs a calibrated CD
  with pointwise frequentist coverage by construction, with no prior
  and no MCMC --- inversion is just root-finding on
  \(\|r(\theta; X_\mathrm{obs})\|^2 \le \chi^2_{d, \alpha}\). For
  Bayesian use cases SNL is the more natural fit; for frequentist
  coverage guarantees CD-SBI is.
\end{itemize}

CD-SBI's specific contribution is therefore the architectural recipe
(monotone in \(\theta\) + monotone in \(X\) + triangular structure for
multivariate) that converts the standard SNL training objective into a
procedure whose output is a frequentist CD by construction (Fig.~\ref{fig:c6}).

\paragraph{Connection to the implicit-likelihood-ratio tradition.}
CD-SBI also relates to the implicit-likelihood-ratio (LR) tradition
\citep{CranmerEtAl2015}, which uses neural classifiers to approximate
likelihood ratios in scientific simulators. In the location-normal
model the score is \(\partial_\theta \log p(X \mid \theta) = X - \theta\),
the standardized log-likelihood gradient; our optimal pivot
\(r^*(\theta; X) = \theta - X\) is its negative, with the sign chosen
to match the (R1) convention \(\partial_\theta r > 0\). Up to this
sign, \(r^*\) is the standardized score. In regular one-parameter
exponential families more generally, the canonical pivot
\(r^* = \Phi^{-1}(1 - F_\theta(T(X)))\) of \S\ref{subsec:5.1} is related
to the \emph{signed-root log-likelihood ratio}: the signed square root
\(\mathrm{sgn}(\hat\theta - \theta) \sqrt{2[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\theta)]}\),
which is asymptotically \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) under \(P_\theta\) and is
the standard frequentist pivot for one-parameter exponential families.
The connection is developed in \citet[ch.~5]{SchwederHjort2016}.

\section*{Part VII — Open questions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Part VII — Open questions}

The framework as developed has clear scope: 1D and modest multivariate
cases (\(d \le 2\) in the experiments), well-specified simulators,
amortized inference, and an autoregressive triangular flow architecture.
Part VII enumerates what remains open beyond that scope, organized by
theme: theoretical extensions (uniqueness outside the autoregressive
class, ordering selection, the \(C^0\) residual in 1D); robustness
under misspecification; scaling and practice (higher dimensions,
sequential variants, learned-feature discrete extensions); and one
implementation challenge (efficient \(C_\alpha\) computation in
\(d \ge 3\)).

\section{Open problems}\label{sec:11}

The eight open problems below group naturally by theme. We mark each
group with a small heading; the §11.X subsection numbering is preserved
for cross-referencing.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Theoretical extensions.}
\medskip

\subsection{Uniqueness beyond the autoregressive triangular class}\label{subsec:11.1}
Theorem A-d (\S\ref{subsec:6.3}) establishes uniqueness within autoregressive
triangular flows: the calibration manifold is the singleton
Knothe--Rosenblatt rearrangement. Outside that class the picture is
murkier. Orthogonal rotations of \(r\) preserve the calibration
constraint \(r \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) since the standard Gaussian
is \(O(d)\)-invariant; under the autoregressive (R1\(^{\mathrm{auto}}\)),
only the coordinate-wise sign-flip subgroup \(\mathbb{Z}_2^d\) of \(O(d)\)
preserves the monotonicity structure (rows of a generic rotation mix
coordinates and destroy strict monotonicity in \(\theta_k\) at fixed
\(\theta_{<k}\)). Under weaker non-autoregressive monotonicity constraints
the residual symmetry group of \(\mathcal{M}\) within the architectural
class --- and whether NF-MLE converges to a canonical pivot inside that
larger orbit --- is open. The practical implication, supported by the
2D correlated experiment, is that \emph{some} triangular ordering must
be chosen architecturally.

\subsection{Ordering selection in KR}\label{subsec:11.2}
When there is no natural
ordering of the coordinates of \(X\), different choices of
autoregressive ordering give different valid pivots, all in
\(\mathcal{M}\), all yielding exact coverage. Whether some orderings
have better finite-sample optimization behavior --- easier-to-learn
conditionals, lower variance gradients --- is an empirical question
worth investigating. The optimal-transport literature
\citep{CarlierEtAl2010} connects KR maps to Brenier maps through an
anisotropic-quadratic-cost continuation (taking the cost weights
\(\lambda_1 \gg \cdots \gg \lambda_d\) as the limit recovers KR), raising
the
\emph{conjecture} that a more isotropic transport target might give
better-behaved finite-sample training; this is unexplored in the CD-SBI
context. We are not aware of any published study comparing KR orderings
on finite-sample training stability in either SBI or general
normalizing-flow contexts.

\subsection{The \(C^0\) residual in 1D}\label{subsec:11.3}
In the \(C^1\) case, Lemma~4.2 derives strict monotonicity in \(X\) from
(R1) + calibration + regularity, so (R3) is automatic. Theorem~A*
requires (R3) as an extra assumption only in the \emph{Lipschitz} case,
where Lemma~4.2's blow-up argument fails (a Lipschitz V-shape gives only
a bounded jump in the pushforward density, not a divergence). Whether
(R3) follows from (R1) + Lipschitz + calibration alone --- without the
uniform lower-bound assumption \(|\partial_X r| \ge c > 0\) --- is open.
Lemma~4.2's generalization (\S\ref{subsec:4.2} remark) covers the
\(C^1\) case for arbitrary continuous positive source; in practice,
architectural enforcement of strict \(X\)-monotonicity (UMNN /
triangular flows) makes the question moot.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Robustness.}
\medskip

\subsection{Misspecification}\label{subsec:11.4}
When the simulator does not exactly generate the data, the calibration
target \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) becomes wrong. Under misspecification,
the population minimizer of NF-MLE is the \emph{forward-KL projection}
(the
\(\mathrm{argmin}_{r \in \mathcal{F}} \mathbb{E}_\rho[\mathrm{KL}(p(\cdot \mid \theta) \,\|\, \hat p_r(\cdot \mid \theta))]\)
direction, called the \(M\)-projection in the modern Bregman-geometry
sense) of the true conditional \(p(\cdot \mid \theta)\) onto the
architectural class. What this object's coverage properties are ---
and whether CD-SBI inherits a degraded but still useful guarantee ---
is open.

Three nearby threads in the SBI literature work on related problems
via mechanisms distinct from the forward-KL projection above:
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \citet{WehenkelEtAl2025} introduce \emph{RoPE} (Robust Posterior
  Estimation), an optimal-transport calibration method that uses a
  small real-world calibration set of \((\theta, X)\) pairs to learn
  the simulator-vs-real misspecification gap. RoPE is a separate
  calibration stage layered on top of any SBI training, rather than a
  modification of the training objective itself.
\item
  \citet{SchmonEtAl2020} introduce \emph{generalized posteriors} in the
  ABC setting --- a family of power-posterior reweightings
  \(\pi(\theta) \propto p(\theta) \cdot \mathcal{L}(\theta)^\eta\) for
  \(\eta \in (0, 1)\), which tempers the influence of a misspecified
  likelihood. The downweighting parameter \(\eta\) trades off bias
  versus robustness.
\item
  \citet{DellaportaEtAl2022} use the \emph{MMD posterior bootstrap}: an
  approximate Bayesian update built on the maximum-mean-discrepancy
  loss between simulated and observed data, which is robust to model
  misspecification by virtue of MMD's continuity in the data-generating
  distribution.
\end{itemize}
A direct coverage-degradation analysis of the forward-KL projection of
\(p\) onto \(\mathcal{F}\) --- the projection that NF-MLE actually
delivers in the misspecified setting --- is, to our knowledge, still
missing.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Scaling and practice.}
\medskip

\subsection{Higher-dimensional scaling}\label{subsec:11.5}
The triangular flow scales linearly in \(d\) for the Jacobian, but
conditioning networks for high-\(k\) components carry growing context
dimension. Autoregressive flows in the SBI literature have been benchmarked at
parameter dimensions up to \(d_\theta \sim 10\) and observation
dimensions up to \(d_X \sim 100\) \citep{LueckmannEtAl2021}, and reach
\(d \sim 100\)+ in general normalizing-flow contexts
\citep{PapamakariosEtAl2017, DurkanEtAl2019}. What is specific to CD-SBI
is the requirement
that the calibration--monotonicity--Jacobian triple be maintained
jointly at scale; whether that triple remains tractable past the 2D
experiments here, and in particular whether sparse autoregressive or
attention-based conditioning is needed at high \(d\), is open.
Empirical validation past \(d = 2\) is a natural next milestone.

\subsection{Sequential / amortized variants}\label{subsec:11.6}
SBI has both amortized (single network for all \(X_\mathrm{obs}\)) and
sequential (refine around a specific \(X_\mathrm{obs}\)) variants.
CD-SBI as developed here is amortized. The status of a sequential
variant splits in two:
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item \emph{Naive sequential CD-SBI (adapting \(\rho\) to concentrate
  near \(X_\mathrm{obs}\)) almost certainly loses pointwise calibration:}
  the adaptive \(\rho\) violates the \(\rho\)-independence-in-the-population-limit
  argument of \S\ref{subsec:1.3}, and \citet{HermansEtAl2022}
  documented empirical miscalibration of all naive sequential variants.
  (\citealp{LueckmannEtAl2021}'s benchmark reports distributional
  metrics --- C2ST, MMD, KSD --- but not coverage, so it does not
  itself document sequential-SBI miscalibration in the frequentist
  sense.)
\item \emph{Whether an importance-reweighted or APT-style corrected
  sequential procedure preserves the calibration guarantee is genuinely
  open.} The closest existing work is \citet{FalkiewiczEtAl2023}
  (Calibrated NPE), which adds a differentiable coverage regularizer to
  a sequential loop but does not prove a coverage guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discrete data with no scalar sufficient statistic}\label{subsec:11.7}
For models where no scalar sufficient statistic exists (e.g., images,
sequences), the discrete extension of \S\ref{subsec:5.7} must be
combined with feature extraction. Whether the Lancaster randomization
argument extends to learned-feature settings is open. The specific
obstruction: under a learned \(T(X) = g_\phi(X)\), \(g_\phi\) is not
sufficient in general, the atom probabilities \(p_\theta(t)\) become
feature-extractor-dependent, and the (R4) order-preservation condition
loses its architectural cleanness because the ordering of \(T\)-values
is itself learned.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Engineering.}
\medskip

\subsection{Efficient computation of \(\mathcal{C}_\alpha\) for
multivariate (implementation challenge, not a research problem)}\label{subsec:11.8}
\(\mathcal{C}_\alpha\) is computationally well-defined via autoregressive
1D root-finding (\S\ref{subsec:6.4}). Three standard approaches are
available: (i) invert \(r(\cdot; X_\mathrm{obs})\) autoregressively to
produce boundary samples; (ii) HMC / SMC / rejection sampling on the
implicit set; (iii) pushforward sampling --- draw \(z\) from
\(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) constrained to the \(\chi^2_d\) ball, then
invert \(r(\cdot; X_\mathrm{obs})(\theta) = z\). Efficient visualization
and reporting in \(d \ge 3\) (especially when only boundary samples or
projections are needed) is an engineering question worth developing in
practice; we list it here for completeness rather than as an unresolved
research question.
